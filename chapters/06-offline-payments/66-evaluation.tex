\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:offline-evaluation}
\suppressfloats[t]

We evaluate whether mobile phones and a secure element can run the protocol
within their hardware and storage limitations, and measure every algorithm
separately in a server-side implementation, for the intermediary, the central
bank, the registration authority and the auditor. We benchmark two versions of
the protocol, with and without the verifiable encryption of the payer's
identifier for the auditor, to show the overhead auditing imposes. The version
without auditing has no audit ciphertext, so the statement of the joint proof
has no $\ciph$, the device does not compute $a_1$, $a_2$ and $\pi_\rho$, and
$\JPOKverify$ does not check the last two equations of
\Cref{prot:bbs-joint-proof}.

The phone and smart card measurements come from the original implementation of
the scheme, written at IBM Research Zurich.\rebekah{cite the original
implementation if it is public: not found on GitHub under IBM, hyperledger-labs
or ale-linux (Sorniotti); ask him or Angelo De Caro} It runs the wallet on a Samsung Galaxy A34
with Android 14 and an iPhone 13 mini with iOS 17.6.1, both with a crypto
library built with Go 1.21.8.
The secure element is a Javacard applet on an NXP smart card running its JCOP 4.5 Java Card operating system,
which the phone reaches over near-field communication (NFC)~\cite{javacard}.
\Cref{fig:offline-withdraw-seq,fig:offline-pay-seq} give the message sequences
of a withdrawal and a payment.

We wrote the server-side implementation in Go with gnark-crypto 0.21.0 over
the BLS12-381 curve, and run it on a MacBook Air with an Apple M2 and 16\,GB of
RAM~\cite{gnark-crypto}. We report medians of $100$ runs, after $10$ warm-up
calls on the server. The interquartile range is within $3\%$ of the median for
most operations, and at most $13\%$ on the phones and the card and $10\%$ on the
server.

Withdrawal and the payer side of a payment both complete in under half a second,
and the smart card authorises a payment in under $400\,\mathrm{ms}$.

\Cref{tab:offline-timings} gives the time of each wallet
step on the phones and the card. The card takes most of the time. Auditing adds $9\,\mathrm{ms}$ on
Android and $2\,\mathrm{ms}$ on iOS to the payer's phone, which is under $3\%$
of the payer's total.

We count hops as the signatures of knowledge a verifier checks, so one hop is
the first payment $p_0$ after withdrawal, or a deposit made straight after
withdrawal. Payee verification grows linearly with the number of hops, since
each hop contributes one joint proof to check, which includes the audit
equations when auditing.

\Cref{fig:offline-hops-times,fig:offline-hops-sizes} show how time and size
grow with the length of the chain. With auditing, the payee verifies $32$ hops in
$883\,\mathrm{ms}$ on Android and $577\,\mathrm{ms}$ on iOS. The auditing
overhead of 10 to 20\% holds from four hops on. Payments grow by $912$ bytes per hop with
auditing and by $688$ bytes without.
