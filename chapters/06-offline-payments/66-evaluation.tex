\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:offline-evaluation}
\suppressfloats[t]

We establish the practicality of the scheme by integrating the protocol into a
mobile wallet and benchmarking its performance on both iOS and Android. We focus
our evaluation on mobile phones and secure elements, to establish whether
they can implement the protocol efficiently given their hardware and storage
limitations.

We also measure every algorithm separately in a server-side implementation, for
the intermediary, the central bank, the registration authority and the auditor.

We implement the protocol on a Samsung Galaxy A34 running Android 14 and an
iPhone 13 mini running iOS 17.6.1, both running a crypto library built with Go
1.21.8\rebekah{confirm Go 1.21.8 for the phone library; it's from the old text and the repo has no record of it}. We benchmark two versions of the protocol, with and without the
verifiable encryption of the payer's identifier for the auditor, to clearly show
the overhead auditing imposes. The smart card is an NXP 1ID white plastic card
with a Smart MX D600 chip and 400\,KiB of available memory, running JCOP 4.5 OS
with NXP's JCOPx extensions at version 1.1.4. Card and phone communicate over
near-field communication (NFC).

A fundamental aspect of a realistic benchmark is the choice of secure element.
We consider four options: software emulation of the secure element in the wallet
app, a TEE-based secure element in the mobile platform, a secure element in a
hardware security module (HSM) of the mobile platform, and an external secure element. We discard
software emulation, since it would present unrealistically good performance by
ignoring both the resource-constrained environment of a real secure element and
the communication overhead of talking to one. We discard TEE-based secure
elements as insufficiently secure, given their wider attack surface and history
of compromise~\cite{TEEsBad}. We discard HSM-based secure elements because those
integrated into Apple and Android platforms do not implement our scheme and are
not extensible. We therefore adopt an external secure element, implemented on
smart cards. We use Javacard, a Java-based framework for smart cards, in which
the protocol running on the secure element is implemented as an applet, a small
application supported by a subset of the Java runtime~\cite{javacard}.

Recall that, with the smart card's help, the payer produces a signature of
knowledge over two commitments, the payer's $\com_i$ and the payee's
$\com_{i+1}$. To prevent double spending, the smart card is programmed to sign
only $(v, \com_i, \com_{i+1})$ triples such that $\com_i$ is the commitment the
card generated when it received the token, and to sign against only a single
destination commitment $\com_{i+1}$ for that token once it is spent. The first
constraint ensures the card signs off payments only for the token last received,
avoiding re-spending of past ones. The second ensures the current token can be
transferred to only one recipient.

Withdrawal starts with the holder scanning their card to obtain values $s_0$ and
$\com_0$, using these to generate a withdrawal request and issuing it to the
REST endpoint of their intermediary over HTTPS. The full message sequence is
given in \Cref{fig:offline-withdraw-seq}. Before the offline payment protocol
can start, payer and payee negotiate the terms of the payment and set up a
Bluetooth Low Energy channel. The full message sequence is given in
\Cref{fig:offline-pay-seq}.

The server-side implementation is written in Go with gnark-crypto 0.21.0 over
the BLS12-381 curve, and runs on a MacBook Air with an Apple M2 and 16\,GB of
RAM~\cite{gnark-crypto}. On the server the secure element is replaced by a stub,
which is not timed. We report medians of $100$ runs, after $10$ warm-up calls on
the server. The interquartile range is within $3\%$ of the median for most
operations, and at most $13\%$ on the phones and the card and $10\%$ on the
server.

Withdrawal and the payer side of a payment both complete in under half a second,
and the smart card authorises a payment in under $400\,\mathrm{ms}$.

\Cref{tab:offline-timings} and \Cref{fig:offline-phone-steps} give the time of each wallet
step on the phones and the card. Most of it is spent on the card, which takes
about $230\,\mathrm{ms}$ to generate a pseudonym and $370\,\mathrm{ms}$ for its
share of the transfer signature, NFC included. Auditing adds $9\,\mathrm{ms}$ on
Android and $2\,\mathrm{ms}$ on iOS to the payer's phone, which is under $3\%$
of the payer's total.

Payee verification grows linearly with the number of hops, since each hop
contributes one signature of knowledge and one audit proof to check, and even a
chain of 32 hops verifies in under a second. Auditing adds a 10 to 20\% overhead
for the payee, who checks one additional proof per hop.

\Cref{fig:offline-hops-times,fig:offline-hops-sizes} show how time and size
grow with the length of the chain. With auditing, the payee verifies $32$ hops in
$883\,\mathrm{ms}$ on Android and $577\,\mathrm{ms}$ on iOS. The auditing
overhead of 10 to 20\% holds from four hops on. Payments grow by $912$ bytes per hop with
auditing and by $688$ bytes without.
