\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:offline-evaluation}
\suppressfloats[t]

We evaluate whether mobile phones and a secure element can run the protocol
within their hardware and storage limitations, and measure every algorithm
separately in a server-side implementation, for the intermediary, the central
bank, the registration authority and the auditor. We benchmark two versions of
the protocol, with and without the verifiable encryption of the payer's
identifier for the auditor, to show the overhead auditing imposes.

The phone and smart card measurements come from the original implementation of
the scheme.\rebekah{cite the original implementation; it is not cited anywhere
yet} It runs the wallet on a Samsung Galaxy A34 with Android 14 and an iPhone 13
mini with iOS 17.6.1, both with a crypto library built with Go
1.21.8\rebekah{confirm Go 1.21.8 for the phone library; it's from the old text and the repo has no record of it},
and the secure element as a Javacard applet~\cite{javacard} on an NXP smart card
running JCOP 4.5, which the phone reaches over near-field communication (NFC).
\Cref{fig:offline-withdraw-seq,fig:offline-pay-seq} give the message sequences
of a withdrawal and a payment.

The server-side implementation is written in Go with gnark-crypto 0.21.0 over
the BLS12-381 curve, and runs on a MacBook Air with an Apple M2 and 16\,GB of
RAM~\cite{gnark-crypto}. We report medians of $100$ runs, after $10$ warm-up
calls on the server. The interquartile range is within $3\%$ of the median for
most operations, and at most $13\%$ on the phones and the card and $10\%$ on the
server.

Withdrawal and the payer side of a payment both complete in under half a second,
and the smart card authorises a payment in under $400\,\mathrm{ms}$.

\Cref{tab:offline-timings} gives the time of each wallet
step on the phones and the card.\rebekah{the text does not say where the phone and card measurements come from (when, which code version, which of the kept json or dataset files); the json files differ from the dataset files the tables use} We count hops as the signatures of knowledge a verifier
checks, so one hop is the first payment $p_0$ after withdrawal, or a deposit
made straight after withdrawal. Most of the time is spent on the card, which takes
about $230\,\mathrm{ms}$ to generate a pseudonym and $370\,\mathrm{ms}$ for its
share of the transfer signature, NFC included. Auditing adds $9\,\mathrm{ms}$ on
Android and $2\,\mathrm{ms}$ on iOS to the payer's phone, which is under $3\%$
of the payer's total.

Payee verification grows linearly with the number of hops, since each hop
contributes one signature of knowledge and one audit proof to check. Even a
chain of 32 hops verifies in under a second. Auditing adds an overhead for the
payee, who checks one additional proof per hop.

\Cref{fig:offline-hops-times,fig:offline-hops-sizes} show how time and size
grow with the length of the chain. With auditing, the payee verifies $32$ hops in
$883\,\mathrm{ms}$ on Android and $577\,\mathrm{ms}$ on iOS. The auditing
overhead of 10 to 20\% holds from four hops on. Payments grow by $912$ bytes per hop with
auditing and by $688$ bytes without.
