\section{Construction}\label{sec:offline-construction}

We first build intuition for the construction by describing, informally, a
sequence of increasingly refined solutions, before giving the protocol in full.

In its simplest form, a token is a triple $\tau = (\pk, v, s)$, where $\pk$ is
the public key of its owner, $v$ is its value, and $s$ is a random number that
uniquely identifies it.

\paragraph{Withdrawals.} To withdraw a token, the user sends their intermediary a
withdrawal request $\langle v, s_0 \rangle$, where $v$ is the value to be
withdrawn and $s_0$ is a random number they select. After checking that the
user has enough funds, the intermediary adds the user's public key $\pk_0$ and
forwards the triple $\langle \pk_0, v, s_0 \rangle$ to the central bank, which
checks that the intermediary has enough funds and responds with a signature
$\psi_0$ on $\langle \pk_0, v, s_0 \rangle$. With $\psi_0$, the user can spend
the token $\tau_0 = (\pk_0, v, s_0)$. A payment in this scheme transfers
ownership of a token from a payer to a payee, and never splits or merges
tokens.

\paragraph{Payments.} To pay the token onward, the payer computes a signature
$\sigma_0$ on $(v, s_0, \pk_0, \pk_1)$, where $\pk_1$ is the payee's public
key, and sends the tuple $\langle v, s_0, \pk_0, \pk_1, \sigma_0, \psi_0
\rangle$. The payee accepts the payment if $\sigma_0$ and $\psi_0$ both verify
against this information, and can subsequently transfer the token onward in
the same way. After $n$ consecutive payments, the final payee receives
$\langle v, s_0, \pk_0, \ldots, \pk_n, \sigma_0, \ldots, \sigma_{n-1}, \psi_0
\rangle$, and accepts it if every signature $\sigma_i$ in the chain is valid on
$(v, s_0, \pk_i, \pk_{i+1})$ under the corresponding public key $\pk_i$, and
$\psi_0$ is a valid central bank signature on $(\pk_0, v, s_0)$.

\paragraph{Deposits.} To deposit the token, its final owner computes a
signature $\sigma_n$ on $(v, s_0, \pk_n)$ and sends $\langle v, s_0, \pk_0,
\ldots, \pk_n, \sigma_0, \ldots, \sigma_n, \psi_0 \rangle$ to their intermediary,
which forwards the deposit to the central bank. The central bank verifies that
$\sigma_n$ is a valid signature on $(v, s_0, \pk_n)$, that $\psi_0$ is a valid
signature of the central bank on $(\pk_0, v, s_0)$, and that every signature
$\sigma_i$ in the chain is valid on $(v, s_0, \pk_i, \pk_{i+1})$ under $\pk_i$.
If all checks pass, the deposit is accepted. If another deposit request
already starts with $(v, s_0, \pk_0)$, the central bank rejects it as double
spending.

\paragraph{Identifying double spenders.} This simple scheme is prone to
framing. Naively, the double spender is identified as the owner of the public
key at the point where two conflicting deposit chains diverge, but this
assumes a double spender never spends the same token twice to the same payee.
A stateful payee who remembers every payment they have been involved in could
reject duplicates, but this is unreasonable to expect. Instead, we have each
payee select a random number that uniquely identifies the payment they are
about to receive, and that is carried forward in subsequent payments,
following an approach also used in transferable
e-cash~\cite{EC:CamHohLys05,CANS:FucPoiVer09,PKC:BCFK15}. This ensures that two
payments from the same payer to the same payee using the same token still
carry distinct random numbers, so a payee who received the same token twice is
not at risk of being framed. Concretely, before paying the token onward the
payer receives a fresh random number $s_{i+1}$ from the payee, computes a
signature $\sigma_i$ on $(v, s_i, s_{i+1}, \pk_i, \pk_{i+1})$, and sends
$\langle v, s_0, \pk_0, s_1, \pk_1, \ldots, s_{i+1}, \pk_{i+1}, \sigma_0,
\ldots, \sigma_i, \psi_0 \rangle$ to the payee. At deposit, the final owner
computes a signature $\sigma_n$ on $(v, s_n, \pk_n)$ and sends the analogous
deposit request. Given two conflicting deposit requests both spending $(v,
s_0, \pk_0)$, the double spender is now identified as the owner of the public
key at the point $j$ where $(\pk_i, s_i) = (\pk'_i, s'_i)$ for all $i \leq j$
but $(\pk_{j+1}, s_{j+1}) \neq (\pk'_{j+1}, s'_{j+1})$: this user provably sent
two distinct payments, one carrying $s_{j+1}$ and the other $s'_{j+1}$.

\paragraph{Adding anonymity.} The scheme so far achieves token unforgeability,
double spending detection, and double spender identification, but not privacy:
the chain of payments reveals every payer's and payee's public key. We add
anonymity by having users obtain long-term credentials from the registration
authority that bind their unique identifier to their secret key. Rather than a
public key, a token's owner is now identified by a pseudonym: a hiding
commitment to the owner's unique identifier, so a token is $\tau = (v, C)$ for
such a commitment $C$. During payments and deposits, the user produces an
anonymous signature by proving in zero knowledge that the committed identifier
was signed by the registration authority, together with a signature of
knowledge that they know the corresponding secret key. This gives a chain of
payments of the form $\langle v, s_0, C_0, \ldots, s_n, C_n, \sigma_0, \ldots,
\sigma_n, \psi_0 \rangle$, where $\psi_0$ now signs $\tau_0 = (C_0, v, s_0)$
rather than $(\pk_0, v, s_0)$. We can further shrink this chain by not
revealing $s_i$ and instead using it directly as the randomness underlying the
commitment $C_i$, giving payments of the form $\langle v, C_0, \ldots, C_n,
\sigma_0, \ldots, \sigma_n, \psi_0 \rangle$.

\paragraph{Adding auditability.} To identify double spenders at deposit time,
each payment carries a verifiable encryption of the payer's unique identifier,
intended for an authorised auditor. Given two deposit requests $\langle v, C_0,
\ciph_0, \ldots, C_n, \ciph_n, \sigma_0, \ldots, \sigma_n, \psi_0 \rangle$ and
$\langle v, C_0, \ciph'_0, \ldots, C'_n, \ciph'_n, \sigma'_0, \ldots,
\sigma'_n, \psi_0 \rangle$ starting from the same $(v, C_0)$, the double
spender is identified by decrypting the ciphertext $\ciph_j$ at the index $j$
where $C_i = C'_i$ for all $i \leq j$ but $C_{j+1} \neq C'_{j+1}$. Since the
auditor can de-anonymise payments, we recommend distributing it via threshold
decryption, so decryption succeeds only if more than a threshold $t$ of
auditors cooperate.

\paragraph{Hiding the link between withdrawals and deposits.} The scheme so far
does not prevent linking a withdrawal to a deposit. This is a genuine privacy
concern even though payments themselves are anonymised, since users identify
themselves to their intermediaries at both withdrawal and deposit: if a token
is transferred only once before being deposited, and the payer's and payee's
intermediaries collude, they can de-anonymise the payment. We avoid this using
a blind signature scheme, which lets the central bank sign the token $\tau_0 =
(v, C_0)$ without learning $C_0$ or the resulting signature $\psi_0$. We note
that the withdrawal and deposit of a token can still be linked through its
value. This can be avoided by using a single denomination, or mitigated by
using fixed denominations together with aggregated deposits, which let a
depositor disclose only the aggregate value of several deposited tokens rather
than the value of each.

\paragraph{Using secure elements to limit double spending.} As discussed in the
introduction, secure elements make double spending attacks impractical to
mount. Since today's secure elements have limited compute and storage, we split
the computation above into a lightweight part executed by the secure element
and a heavier part offloaded to the user's mobile device.  The secure element
is responsible for three tasks: generating the randomness $s_i$ and computing
the commitment $C_i$, producing the signature of knowledge of the user's secret
key, which only it can access, and deleting a token once it has been spent. The
mobile device handles everything else, in particular verifying received
payments, computing the verifiable encryption, and generating the
zero-knowledge proofs that show the user is enrolled in the system.

\paragraph{Privacy guarantees.} To summarise, the scheme gives three
guarantees. First, transactors are anonymous to anyone who sees a transaction
transcript, assuming payer and payee exchange identities out of band. Second,
withdrawals and deposits are unlinkable to every party in the system, so no
one can trace a deposit or payment to its origin or destination from the
payment transcripts. Third, the auditor may deanonymise payments, and is
trusted to do so only with evidence of double spending, with the auditor role
recommended to be distributed via threshold decryption.

We now describe the complete protocol formally, stated generically with respect
to the underlying commitment scheme, digital signature scheme, blind signature
scheme, and public key encryption scheme. \Cref{sec:offline-instantiation}
instantiates each of them.

\begin{protocolbox}{Offline payment protocol}{offline}

  \begin{description}

    \item[$(\pk_{\algo{cb}}, \ck, \pk_{\auditor}, \rapk) \leftarrow
      \OPgen(\secparam)$ :]\label{sec:offline:construction:setup} On input
      security parameter $\secparam$, do:

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item The central bank generates keys for a blind signature scheme
          supporting three-message vectors, obtaining $(\sk_{\algo{cb}},
          \pk_{\algo{cb}})$.

        \item The central bank runs $\ck \leftarrow \ComGen(\secparam)$ to
          obtain a public commitment key.

        \item The auditor generates an asymmetric encryption key pair
          $(\sk_{\auditor}, \pk_{\auditor}) \leftarrow \PKEgen(\secparam)$.

        \item The registration authority generates keys for a multi-message
          signature scheme supporting two-message vectors, obtaining $(\rask,
          \rapk) \leftarrow \DSgen(\secparam, 2)$.

        \item Publish all public keys and generators to every participant, and
          return $(\pk_{\algo{cb}}, \ck, \pk_{\auditor}, \rapk)$.

      \end{enumerate}

    \item[$\cred \leftarrow \OPregister(\rask)$ :] When a user opens an
      account, do the following:

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item Provision the user's secure element $\algo{se}$ with a fresh
          secret key $\sk_{\algo{usr}}$ and a unique random identifier
          $\algo{uid}$.

        \item The registration authority verifies that $\algo{uid}$ is fresh
          and that $\algo{se}$ knows $\sk_{\algo{usr}}$, via an interactive
          zero-knowledge proof of knowledge.

        \item The registration authority issues credential $\cred =
          (\algo{uid}, \pk_{\algo{usr}}, \varphi)$ with $\varphi \leftarrow
          \DSsign(\rask, (\algo{uid}, \sk_{\algo{usr}}))$.

        \item Pair the user's mobile device $\algo{dev}$ with $\algo{se}$, so
          that only $\algo{dev}$ can send instructions to $\algo{se}$.

        \item Return $\cred$.

      \end{enumerate}

    \item[$T_0 \leftarrow \OPwithdraw(v_0)$ :]\label{sec:withdrawal} The user
      withdraws a token of value $v_0$. Do the following:

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item The user's mobile device instructs their secure element to
          initiate a withdrawal of value $v_0$.

        \item The secure element selects a random $s_0$, computes $\com_0
          \leftarrow \com(\ck, \algo{uid}_0; s_0)$, and returns $(s_0, \com_0)$
          to the device.

        \item The device computes $\com \leftarrow \com(\ck, (\algo{uid}_0,
          v_0); s_0)$.

        \item The device prepares a blind signature request on the committed
          vector $(\algo{uid}_0, v_0, s_0)$, hiding $\algo{uid}_0$ and $s_0$
          and disclosing $v_0$, together with a zero-knowledge proof $\Pi$
          that $\com$ is correctly formed and that the accompanying
          ciphertexts are correct, and sends $\algo{req} = (\algo{uid}_0, \com,
          \ciph_0, v_0, \ciph_2, \Pi, \rho_0)$ to the intermediary.

        \item The intermediary verifies $\Pi$, locks $v_0$ in the user's
          account, and forwards $(\com, \ciph_0, v_0, \ciph_2, \Pi)$ to the
          central bank.

        \item The central bank checks that $\com$ is fresh, verifies $\Pi$,
          debits the intermediary's reserves, and returns a blind signature
          response $\algo{resp}$.

        \item The device unblinds $\algo{resp}$ to obtain a signature $\psi
          \leftarrow \DSsign(\sk_{\algo{cb}}, (\algo{uid}_0, v_0); s_0)$ and
          stores $(v_0, s_0, \com_0, \psi)$. The secure element stores the
          original token $\tau_0 = (v_0, \com_0)$.

        \item The device produces a proof of withdrawal $\Sigma$ with
          $\inst = (\ck, \pk_{\algo{cb}}, v_0, \com_0)$, $\witness =
          (\algo{uid}_0, s_0, \psi)$, and relation as follows:
          \begin{equation*} \DSverify(\pk_{\algo{cb}}, (\algo{uid}_0, v_0);
            s_0, \psi) = 1 \;\wedge\; \ComOpen(\ck, \algo{uid}_0; s_0, \com_0)
          = 1.  \end{equation*}

        \item Return $T_0 = (v_0, s_0, \com_0, \algo{hist}_0 = \Sigma)$.

      \end{enumerate}

    \item[$p_i \leftarrow \OPexecute(v, s_i, \com_i, \algo{hist}_i)$
      :]\label{sec:payments} The payer pays the payee using token $(v, s_i,
      \com_i, \algo{hist}_i)$. Do the following:

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item The payee's device instructs their secure element to prepare to
          receive a payment of value $v$.

        \item The payee's secure element selects a random $s_{i+1}$, computes
          $\com_{i+1} \leftarrow \com(\ck, \algo{uid}_{i+1}; s_{i+1})$, and
          returns $(s_{i+1}, \com_{i+1})$ to their device.

        \item The payee's device sends $\com_{i+1}$ to the payer's device.

        \item The payer's device notifies their secure element that a payment is
          under way. The secure element locks token $\tau_i = (v, \com_i)$.

        \item The payer's device computes the audit ciphertext $\ciph_i
          \leftarrow \PKEencrypt(\pk_{\auditor}, \algo{uid}_i; \rho_i)$ and a
          proof of correctness $\Gamma_i$, with $\inst = (\com_i, \ciph_i)$,
          $\witness = (\algo{uid}_i, s_i, \rho_i)$, and relation as follows:
          \begin{equation*} \ComOpen(\ck, \algo{uid}_i, s_i, \com_i) = 1
          \;\wedge\; \ciph_i = \PKEencrypt(\pk_{\auditor}, \algo{uid}_i;
        \rho_i).  \end{equation*}

      \item The payer's secure element and device jointly compute a signature
        of knowledge $\sigma_i$ for the relation as follows: \begin{equation*}
          \DSverify(\rapk, \algo{uid}_i, \sk_{\algo{usr}_i}, \varphi_i) = 1
          \;\wedge\; \ComOpen(\ck, \algo{uid}_i, s_i, \com_i) = 1.
        \end{equation*} Here, the secure element contributes knowledge of the
        payer's secret key and the device contributes the remaining witness.

      \item The payer's device sends payment $p_i = (v, \com_i, \com_{i+1},
        \ciph_i, \sigma_i, \Gamma_i, \algo{hist}_i)$ to the payee's device.

      \item The payee's device verifies $\algo{hist}_i$: if $i = 0$, it checks
        $\Sigma$. Otherwise it verifies every $(\Sigma, \Gamma_k, \sigma_k)_{k
        \in [i]}$.

      \item If $\Gamma_i$ and $\sigma_i$ also verify, the payee's device
        accepts and stores $T_{i+1} = (v, s_{i+1}, \com_{i+1},
        \algo{hist}_{i+1} = (\com_i, \ciph_i, \sigma_i, \Gamma_i) \, \| \,
        \algo{hist}_i)$, and their secure element stores $\tau_{i+1} = (v,
        \com_{i+1})$.

      \item Upon acknowledgement, the payer's secure element deletes $\com_i$,
        and their device returns $p_i$.

    \end{enumerate}

  \item[$\{0,1\} \leftarrow \OPdeposit(\tau_n, \algo{hist}_n)$
    :]\label{sec:deposit} The user deposits token $\tau_n = (v, \com_n)$. Do
    the following:

    \begin{enumerate}

      \item The user instructs their device to deposit $\tau_n$. The device
        informs the secure element to lock $\tau_n$.

      \item The device and secure element jointly compute a signature of
        knowledge $\sigma_n$ over $\tau_n$, structured analogously to the
        signature $\sigma_i$ computed during $\OPexecute$.

      \item The device sends deposit request $d_n = (\tau_n, \sigma_n,
        \algo{hist}_n)$ to the intermediary, which forwards it to the central
        bank.

      \item The central bank verifies $\sigma_n$ and $\algo{hist}_n$. If either
        check fails, return $0$.

      \item Otherwise, the central bank checks whether any prior deposit shares
        the same origin $\com_0$. If so, it records double spending, notifies
        the auditor, and returns $0$.

      \item Otherwise, the central bank credits the intermediary's account,
        which in turn credits the user's account, and the secure element
        deletes $\tau_n$ upon notification.

      \item Return $1$.

    \end{enumerate}

  \item[$\algo{uid}^* \leftarrow \OPidentify(d_n, d_m)$ :] On input two
    conflicting deposit requests $d_n$ and $d_m$ involving the same original
    token, the auditor does:

    \begin{enumerate}

      \item Parse $\algo{hist}_n = ((\com_k, \ciph_k, \sigma_k, \Gamma_k)_{k
        \in [n]}, \Sigma)$ and $\algo{hist}_m$ analogously.

      \item Find the divergence index $j$ such that $\com_k = \com'_k$ for all
        $k \leq j$ but $\com_{j+1} \neq \com'_{j+1}$.

      \item Decrypt $\ciph_j$ (or $\ciph'_j$) using $\sk_{\auditor}$ to recover
        $\algo{uid}^*$, the identifier of the owner of $\tau_j = (v, \com_j)$.

      \item Return $\algo{uid}^*$.

    \end{enumerate}

\end{description}

\end{protocolbox}

Revocation must not leak
information about a user's past transactions, and it is difficult to address with
the inherent lack of verified real time information of the offline setting. A payee who goes offline
just before a payer is revoked will accept the
revoked payer's token. A useful mitigation is an expiry policy organised into epochs, capping either
spending hops or time since withdrawal. Payees, not spenders, enforce this by
rejecting payments outside the current window, tuned to transaction value.
 Capping individual transaction values is a
complementary mitigation, together making double spending financially
unattractive even before detection.

Users must never hold
$\sk_{\algo{usr}}$ directly. With direct access, they can produce unlimited
payments from the same token, since detecting separate double spendings
is inherently impossible offline. The secure elements and key-splitting techniques
 support double spending resistance but cannot
enforce it. The gap is then left to governance.

Use of secure elements and inaccessible keys requires a
recovery mechanism for lost devices.
Other techniques are then needed to understand and address this without sacrificing usability.

Mobile TEEs protect app secrets
from a malicious owner but expose them to developers only via key-management
APIs~\cite{trustzone,secureenclave}. Key confidentiality doesn't guarantee
logic integrity, so arbitrary code execution could still
enable repeated spending via the key manager. Smart cards (Javacard, MIFARE)
instead protect both the key and logic with tamper resistant, isolated
execution~\cite{javacard,mifare}. No hardware security is unconditional, so
deployment involves trade offs between infrastructure and equipment costs,
system wide value limits, and the cost of attacking a card.
