\section{Instantiations}\label{sec:prelims-inst}

This section gives the setting, assumptions and concrete schemes that the rest
of the thesis builds on. It includes the standard instantiations of primitives
from this chapter, the Pedersen commitment scheme and the ElGamal encryption
scheme, and the BBS+ signature scheme, which more than one chapter uses.

\subsection{Groups and assumptions}\label{sec:prelims-groups}

For a prime $p$, we write $\Zp$ for the integers modulo $p$, and call its
elements field elements. We write $\Zp^*$ for $\Zp \setminus \{0\}$. We use
cyclic groups of prime order $p$, written multiplicatively, and call their
elements group elements. For a generator $\gen$ and $a = \gen^x$, the field
element $x \in \Zp$ is the discrete logarithm of $a$ relative to $\gen$.

We use three such groups, $\group{1}$, $\group{2}$, and
$\group{T}$ of prime order $p$, with generators $\gen \in \group{1}$ and $\ggen
\in \group{2}$. The groups are equipped with an efficiently computable pairing
$\pairing \colon \group{1} \times \group{2} \rightarrow \group{T}$. For all $a
\in \group{1}$, $\widetilde{b} \in \group{2}$, and $x,y \in \Zp$, bilinearity
means that the following holds:
%
\begin{equation*}
%
  \pairing(a^x,\widetilde{b}^y) = \pairing(a,\widetilde{b})^{xy}.
%
\end{equation*}
%
The pairing is non-degenerate, so $\pairing(\gen,\ggen)$ generates $\group{T}$.
We call $\group{1}$ and $\group{2}$ the source groups and $\group{T}$ the target
group. We work in the asymmetric, or type III, setting, in which $\group{1} \neq
\group{2}$ and no efficiently computable homomorphism between them is known.
We write elements of $\group{2}$ with a tilde.

Bilinearity allows relations between exponents in the source groups to be
checked through equalities in $\group{T}$. The equality
$\pairing(a,\ggen) = \pairing(\gen,\widetilde{b})$ shows that $a$ and
$\widetilde{b}$ have the same discrete logarithm relative to their generators,
without that discrete logarithm being recovered. A pairing product equation is
an equation of the form $\prod_j \pairing(a_j, \widetilde{b}_j) = t$ for $t \in
\group{T}$.

We first state the discrete logarithm and Decisional Diffie-Hellman assumptions in \Cref{asm:dl} and
\Cref{asm:ddh}~\cite{DifHel76,Boneh98}.

\begin{assumptionbox}{The discrete logarithm (DL) assumption~\cite{DifHel76}.}{dl}

  The DL assumption holds in a group of prime order $p$ with generator $\gen$
if, for $x \sample \Zp$, no PPT adversary given $\gen^x$ outputs $x$ with
non-negligible probability.

\end{assumptionbox}

\begin{assumptionbox}{The Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) assumption~\cite{Boneh98}.}{ddh}

  The DDH assumption holds in a group of prime order $p$ with generator $\gen$
if $(\gen^x, \gen^y, \gen^{xy})$ and $(\gen^x, \gen^y, \gen^z)$, for $x, y, z
\sample \Zp$, are computationally indistinguishable.

\end{assumptionbox}

The symmetric external Diffie-Hellman (SXDH) assumption holds in a bilinear group
if DDH holds in both $\group{1}$ and
$\group{2}$~\cite{EPRINT:BGMM05,EPRINT:ACHM05}.

DDH cannot hold in a symmetric bilinear group, with $\group{1} = \group{2}$, or
in a source group with an efficiently computable homomorphism to the other. SXDH
requires the type III setting.

Some schemes we use are only proven secure in the generic group model
(GGM)~\cite{EC:Shoup97,IMA:Maurer05}. In the GGM, the adversary sees only
opaque handles to group elements, and computes on them only through oracles for
the group operations and the pairing. A proof in the GGM rules out attacks that
ignore the representation of the group, but gives a weaker guarantee than a
reduction to a stated assumption. Some assumptions are $q$-type, meaning their
statement grows with a parameter $q$, typically the number of signing queries.

\subsection{The Pedersen commitment scheme}

The Pedersen commitment scheme in \Cref{prim:pedersen} commits to a field
element with a single group element~\cite{C:Pedersen91}. It is perfectly hiding,
and computationally binding under the DL assumption. Binding holds only against
parties that know no discrete logarithm relation between the elements of $\ck$.
Where a participant could otherwise choose $\ck$, our instantiations derive it
from a public seed with a hash function.

\begin{primitivebox}{The Pedersen commitment scheme~\cite{C:Pedersen91}.}{pedersen}

  \begin{description}

    \item[$\ck \sample \ComGen(\secparam)$ :] On input the security parameter,
sample $\gen_1, \hgen \sample \group{1}$ and return $\ck = (\gen_1, \hgen)$.

    \item[$\com \leftarrow \ComCommit(\ck,\msg,\openinfo)$ :] On input
commitment key $\ck$, message $\msg \in \Zp$, and opening $\openinfo \in \Zp$,
return $\com = \gen_1^{\msg}\cdot\hgen^{\openinfo}$.

  \end{description}

\end{primitivebox}

The scheme extends to vectors of $n$ field elements with one generator per
element. The key is $\ck = (\gen_1, \ldots, \gen_n, \hgen) \sample
\ComGen(\secparam, n)$, and a commitment to $(\msg_1, \ldots, \msg_n)$ is the
following:
%
\begin{equation*}
  \com = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \gen_i^{\msg_i} \cdot \hgen^{\openinfo}.
\end{equation*}
%
It is perfectly hiding and binding under the DL assumption in the same way.

\subsection{The ElGamal encryption scheme}

The ElGamal encryption scheme in \Cref{prim:elgamal} encrypts group
elements~\cite{C:ElGamal84}. In $\group{1}$, it is IND-CPA secure under the DDH
assumption in $\group{1}$. We encrypt field elements $m$ as $\gen^m$, and
decryption gives back $\gen^m$ rather than $m$.

\begin{primitivebox}{The ElGamal encryption scheme~\cite{C:ElGamal84}.}{elgamal}

  \begin{description}

    \item[$(\sk, \pk) \sample \PKEgen(\secparam)$ :] Sample $\mu \sample \Zp$,
and return $\sk = \mu$ and $\pk = \gen^{\mu}$.

    \item[$\ciph \sample \PKEencrypt(\pk, M)$ :] On input $M \in \group{1}$,
sample $\rho \sample \Zp$, and return $\ciph = (\gen^{\rho}, M \pk^{\rho})$.

    \item[$M \leftarrow \PKEdecrypt(\sk, \ciph)$ :] On input $\ciph = (f_1, f_2)$,
return $f_2 / f_1^{\mu}$.

  \end{description}

\end{primitivebox}

\subsection{The BBS+ signature scheme}\label{sec:prelims-bbs}

The BBS+ signature scheme of \Cref{prim:bbs} signs several messages at
once~\cite{C:BonBoySha04,SCN:AuSusMu06}.

\begin{primitivebox}{The BBS+ signature scheme~\cite{C:BonBoySha04}.}{bbs}

  \begin{description}

    \item[$(\sk,\pk) \sample \BBSgen(\secparam, n, (\gen_0,\ldots,\gen_{n+1},
\ggen))$ :] On input the security parameter, vector length $n$, and generators
$\gen_0,\gen_1, \ldots, \gen_{n+1} \in \group{1}$, $\ggen \in \group{2}$, do the
following:

      \begin{enumerate}

	\item Sample $z \sample \Zp$.

	\item Compute $\ggen_Z = \ggen^z$.

	\item Return $\sk = z$ and $\pk = (\gen_0, \ldots, \gen_{n+1}, \ggen,
\ggen_Z)$.

      \end{enumerate}

    \item[$\signature \sample \BBSsign(\sk,(\msg_1, \ldots, \msg_n))$ :] On
input secret key $\sk = z$ and message $(\msg_1, \ldots, \msg_n) \in
\Zp^n$, do the following:

      \begin{enumerate}

	\item Sample $e,r \sample \Zp$.

	\item Compute $E$ as follows:
	%
	\begin{equation*}
	%
	  E = \Big(\gen_0 \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n} \gen_i^{\msg_i} \cdot
\gen_{n+1}^{r}\Big)^{1/(z+e)}.
	%
	\end{equation*}

	\item Return $\signature = (e, r, E)$.

      \end{enumerate}

    \item[$\{0,1\} \leftarrow \BBSverify(\pk,(\msg_1, \ldots, \msg_n),
\signature)$ :] On input public key $\pk$, message $(\msg_1, \ldots, \msg_n)$,
and signature $\signature = (e,r,E)$, do the following:

      \begin{enumerate}

	\item Return $1$ if and only if the following holds:
	%
	\begin{equation*}
	%
	  \pairing(E,\ggen_Z\cdot\ggen^{e}) = \pairing\Big(\gen_0 \cdot
\prod_{i=1}^{n} \gen_i^{\msg_i} \cdot \gen_{n+1}^{r}, \ggen\Big).
	%
	\end{equation*}

      \end{enumerate}

  \end{description}

\end{primitivebox}

The generators are an input to key generation. Standard presentations have
the signer sample them, which is sound when the signer is the only party relying
on them. Where the same generators are also the key of a commitment whose binding must hold
against the signer, they are instead fixed by hashing to $\group{1}$ from a
public seed, so that no party knows a discrete logarithm relation among them.

The BBS+ signature scheme on field elements is existentially unforgeable under
the $q$-strong Diffie-Hellman ($q$-SDH) assumption of \Cref{asm:qsdh-bbs}, a $q$-type
assumption~\cite{EC:BonBoy04a}.

\begin{assumptionbox}{$q$-Strong Diffie-Hellman ($q$-SDH)}{qsdh-bbs}

  The $q$-SDH assumption holds in the type III bilinear group
$(\group{1},\group{2},\group{T},\pairing)$ if, given $(\gen, \gen^{\theta},
\ldots, \gen^{\theta^{q}}, \ggen, \ggen^{\theta})$ for $\gen \in \group{1}$,
$\ggen \in \group{2}$ and uniformly random $\theta \sample \Zp$, no PPT
adversary can output a pair $\big(\varsigma,
\gen^{1/(\theta+\varsigma)}\big)$ for any $\varsigma \in \Zp \setminus
\{-\theta\}$ with non-negligible probability.

\end{assumptionbox}

The signer only needs the product $\prod_i \gen_i^{\msg_i}$, not the field
elements themselves. This means a signer can also sign group elements $M_1, \ldots,
M_k$ given by a user, using their product in place of $\prod_i
\gen_i^{\msg_i}$, and produce a signature on committed values without ever
learning them~\cite{SCN:AuSusMu06,EC:TesZhu23b}. Verification is then the
following:
%
\begin{equation*}
  \pairing(E,\ggen_Z\cdot\ggen^{e}) = \pairing\Big(\gen_0 \cdot
\prod_{i=1}^{k} M_i \cdot \gen_{n+1}^{r}, \ggen\Big).
\end{equation*}
%
This variant is malleable, as a signature on one tuple of group elements is also a
signature on any other tuple with the same product. It is unforgeable only if
the signer signs elements that come with a proof of knowledge of their
representation in the bases $\gen_1, \ldots, \gen_n$, from which the reduction
to $q$-SDH extracts the field elements~\cite{SCN:AuSusMu06,EC:TesZhu23b}.

\subsection{Sigma protocols and the Fiat-Shamir transform}\label{sec:prelims-sigma}

Most proof systems in this thesis are Sigma protocols made non-interactive. A
Sigma protocol for a relation $\rel$, with a statement $\inst$ known to both
parties and a witness $\witness$ known to the prover, has three moves. The prover sends a first
message $a$, the verifier replies with a uniform challenge $c$, and the prover
sends a response $z$. The verifier then checks $z$ against $(\inst, a, c)$.
Completeness is defined like the correctness of proof systems, and requires that
proofs of true statements verify. Special soundness
requires an efficient extractor that computes a witness from any two accepting
transcripts $(a, c, z)$ and $(a, c', z')$ with $c \neq c'$. Special
honest-verifier zero knowledge (SHVZK) requires an efficient simulator that,
given $\inst$ and $c$, outputs $(a, z)$ distributed as in an honest run with
challenge $c$~\cite{C:CraDamSch94}.

The Sigma protocol for proof of knowledge of a discrete logarithm proves
knowledge of $x$ with $h = \gen^x$~\cite{JC:Schnorr91}. Sigma protocols for
discrete logarithms are also called Schnorr proofs.
The prover samples $r \sample \Zp$, sends $a$, receives a challenge $c
\sample \Zp$, and returns $z$, computed as follows:
%
\begin{equation*}
%
  a = \gen^r, \qquad z = r + cx.
%
\end{equation*}
%
The verifier accepts if and only if the following holds:
%
\begin{equation*}
%
  \gen^z = a \cdot h^c.
%
\end{equation*}
%
The same protocol proves knowledge of a representation $h = \prod_i
\gen_i^{x_i}$, with one response per exponent. We prove several equations over the same witness by
running the protocol for each in parallel with one shared challenge, so that
they hold together. In this way we can show, for example, that two group
elements have the same discrete logarithm.

The Fiat-Shamir transform makes a Sigma protocol non-interactive. The prover
computes the challenge itself as $c = \hash(\inst, a, \lbl)$, where $\hash$ is a
random oracle and $\lbl$ a label~\cite{C:FiaSha86}. A Sigma protocol has
quasi-unique responses if, for given $(\inst, a, c)$, no efficient prover finds
two distinct accepting responses. It has unique responses if, for given $(\inst,
a, c)$, at most one response is accepting, as in the protocol above. If the
whole statement is hashed, a Sigma protocol with special soundness, SHVZK and
quasi-unique responses gives a simulation extractable labelled proof system in
the random oracle model~\cite{INDOCRYPT:FKMV12}. The hash function takes
group elements and field elements as input, through a fixed encoding of each
into bit strings.

In the random oracle model, the definitions of \Cref{sec:prelims-nizks} are read
as follows~\cite{CCS:BelRog93,INDOCRYPT:FKMV12}. The common reference string
is empty, and the trapdoor of $\NIZKsimgen$ is the ability to program the
random oracle. The simulator samples the challenge and the responses, solves the
verification equations for the first message, and programs the random oracle at
the hashed point to return that challenge. The extractor $\NIZKextract$ is not
straight-line. It rewinds the prover to the hash query that fixed the
challenge, answers it with a fresh challenge, and extracts the witness from the
two accepting transcripts by special soundness. This relies on the forking
lemma~\cite{JC:PoiSte00}. The extractor then depends on the adversary, and
extraction loses a polynomial factor in the number of hash queries. For this
reason, the security proofs of later chapters are in a model where rewinding is
allowed. Online extraction,
as given by the transform of Fischlin, avoids rewinding at the cost of larger
proofs~\cite{C:Fischlin05}.
