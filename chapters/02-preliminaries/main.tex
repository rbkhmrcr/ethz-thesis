\chapter{Preliminaries}\label{chap:preliminaries}

This chapter describes how we define and prove security.
It defines the abstract primitives that later chapters build
on: commitment schemes, pseudorandom functions, hash functions, signature
schemes, public key encryption schemes and proof systems. These follow Katz
and Lindell wherever possible~\cite{KatLin14}. We then instantiate these
primitives, first defining bilinear groups and the assumptions we rely on. We
then give the Pedersen commitment scheme, the ElGamal encryption scheme, the
BBS+ signature scheme, and Sigma protocols with the Fiat-Shamir transform. Our
notation is listed on page~\pageref{sec:notation}.

Numerous primitives, protocols, security definitions, instantiations and
assumptions appear in this thesis. To ease understanding of them, we use
different coloured boxed environments. Abstract primitives are given directly in
prose, and ideal functionalities as plain figures. Security experiments are in
green boxes, assumptions in pink boxes, instantiations of primitives in blue
boxes, and the protocols that are contributions of this work in purple boxes.
Once experiments, assumptions, primitives and protocols have been introduced,
later chapters refer back to them rather than restating them. Each chapter aims
to otherwise be as self-contained as possible.

\section{Defining secure protocols}\label{sec:prelims-security}

The contributions of this thesis include constructions of protocols and of their
underlying primitives. We take different approaches to defining and proving the
security of protocols and primitives, described below.

Defining the security goals that we wish to achieve requires defining the
capabilities of the adversary and its success probability. There are two main ways of doing this: the concrete approach and
the asymptotic approach. The concrete approach assigns numbers to the amount of
operations that the adversary is able to execute and a fixed bound to the
probability of success. The asymptotic approach instead treats security as a
function parameterised by a security parameter. As this parameter grows, the
resources needed to break a scheme grow faster than any fixed polynomial, and
the probability of success of the adversary becomes vanishingly small.

We have chosen to follow the asymptotic approach, as do most of the works we
build on. This deserves some consideration, as we explicitly aim for concrete
efficiency. We address efficiency through the instantiations, whose elements are
chosen for their efficiency benefits, and their benchmarks. We keep these
choices separate from the design and security proofs of the abstract
constructions.

\paragraph{Adversaries.} We call an adversary efficient if it runs in
probabilistic polynomial time (PPT) in the security parameter. We call this
parameter $\secparam$, and so the adversary's running time is bounded by
$p(\secparam)$ for some fixed polynomial $p$.
Honest parties must also satisfy this definition of efficiency.

For the success probability of the adversary, we define a negligible
function. A function $f$ from the natural
numbers to non-negative real numbers is negligible if it is eventually below every
inverse polynomial. Concretely, for every
polynomial $p$, there exists $N$ such that $f(\secparam) < 1/p(\secparam)$ for
all $\secparam > N$. We write $\negl(\secparam)$ for a negligible
function.

A distinguisher is a PPT adversary that receives a sample from one of two
distributions and outputs a single bit, guessing which one it came from. Two
families of distributions indexed by $\secparam$ are computationally
indistinguishable if no distinguisher guesses correctly with probability
non-negligibly better than $1/2$.

\paragraph{Security of primitives.} We refer to schemes with two or fewer
roles, such as commitments and signature schemes, as primitives, and to those
with many roles as protocols.

An experiment is a game between a challenger,
which runs the honest algorithms, and the adversary $\adv$. The adversary may be
given oracle access, in which it submits inputs and the challenger responds. The advantage $\Adv$ of $\adv$ is its probability of
winning or, in experiments where $\adv$ guesses a bit, the distance of that probability from $1/2$. A property holds if every PPT adversary has negligible advantage.

\paragraph{Security of protocols.} For larger protocols with many parties
and interfaces, we capture all security goals inside one ideal functionality.
The ideal functionality can be thought of as an incorruptible trusted party
receiving the inputs of each protocol task and returning outputs. The view of
parties consists of their inputs, their randomness and the messages they
receive. In the ideal execution, honest parties communicate only with
$\idfu{}$, and a simulator $\simu$ takes the place of $\adv$.

We capture the outputs of honest parties and~$\adv$ during an execution of a
protocol~$\prot$ in the real world in
$\textsc{Exec}_{\prot,\adv}(\secparam)$, and the outputs of the honest
parties and~$\simu$ interacting with an ideal functionality~$\idfu{}$ in the
ideal world by $\textsc{Exec}_{\idfu{},\simu}(\secparam)$. We say the
protocol~$\prot$ securely realises the ideal functionality~$\idfu{}$ if for every
PPT adversary~$\adv$, there exists a PPT simulator~$\simu$ where
$\textsc{Exec}_{\prot,\adv}(\secparam)$ is computationally indistinguishable from
$\textsc{Exec}_{\idfu{},\simu}(\secparam)$. As the ideal functionality achieves
all security properties by design, this shows that the protocol achieves them
too. This definition is in the standalone model: it considers a single
execution of the protocol, with no other protocols running alongside it.

\paragraph{Corruption models.} Honest but curious participants follow the protocol, and the adversary sees
their views and combines them with the views of the other corrupted
participants. Malicious participants deviate from the protocol arbitrarily.
Under static corruption, the set of corrupted participants is fixed before the
protocol starts. Under adaptive corruption, the adversary may corrupt further
participants during the execution, based on what it has seen.
