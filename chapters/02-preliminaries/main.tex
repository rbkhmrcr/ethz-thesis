\chapter{Preliminaries}\label{chap:preliminaries}

The contributions of this thesis include both constructions of protocols and
constructions of their underlying primitives. We take different approaches to
defining and proving the security of each of these.

By primitives, we refer to building blocks such as commitments and signature
schemes.  Security in this case is handled through defining
experiments, a limited number of oracles which an adversary has access to, and
then showing that the adversary cannot win with greater probability than some
predefined amount. For larger protocols with many parties and interfaces, we
capture all security goals at once inside an ideal functionality. We then
construct a simulator to show that the view of an adversary in the real world,
interacting with our protocol, is indistinguishable from the view when
interacting with the ideal functionality itself. As the ideal functionality
achieves all security properties by design, this then shows that the protocol
achieves them too.

The definitions of the security goals that we wish to achieve are not complete
without also defining the capabilities the adversary and the probability of
success that we class as too large. There are two main ways of doing this: the
concrete approach and the asymptotic approach. The concrete approach assigns
numbers to the amount of operations that the adversary is able to execute and a
fixed bound to the probability of success. The asymptotic approach instead
treats security as a function parameterised by a security parameter. Then as
this parameter grows, the resources needed to break a scheme grow faster than
any fixed polynomial, and the probability of success of the adversary becomes
vanishingly small.

We have chosen to follow the asymptotic approach. This deserves some
consideration, as we are explicitly aiming for concrete efficiency in the
design of our protocols and primitives. We wish for the schemes to be used in
practice. The primitives and protocols we design have elements chosen in their
instantiations specifically for the efficiency benefits. However, we wish to
separate the instantiation choices from the design and security proofs of the
abstract constructions that we present.

For asymptotic security definitions, there are a few more details we must give.
An adversary is considered efficient if it runs in probabilistic polynomial
time (PPT) in the security parameter. We call this parameter $\secparam$, and
so the adversary's running time is bounded by $p(\secparam)$ for some fixed
polynomial $p$. It may use randomness in its computation. Honest parties must
also satisfy this definition of efficiency.

For the success probability of the adversary, we define small with a negligible
function. A function $f$ from the natural numbers to non-negative real numbers
is negligible if it is below every inverse polynomial at every point after
reaching some value. Concretely, for every polynomial $p$, there exists $N$
such that $f(\secparam) < 1/p(\secparam)$ for all $\secparam > N$.  Negligible
functions have a property of closure which carries well to their use in
security definitions in cryptography. This is that a polynomial number of
events each occurring with negligible probability, still occurs with negligible
probability in total. This means an adversary repeating an attack with
negligible success probability polynomially many times will still have a
negligible probability of success.

\paragraph{A note on readability.} The thesis contains numerous
primitives, protocols, security definitions, instantiations, and assumptions to
keep track of. To ease the understanding of our contributions, and clearly
separate them from prior work from which we build, a number of different
coloured boxed environments are used.

Abstract primitives are given directly in prose. Security experiments are boxed
in green, to set the formal games apart from the prose around them.
Assumptions are given in pink boxes, and ideal functionalities are displayed as
plain figures. Instantiations of primitives are displayed in blue boxes, and the
protocols that are contributions of this work are given in purple boxes. Once
experiments, assumptions, primitives and protocols have been introduced, later
chapters will refer back to them when needed. Protocols, explanations, and
security analyses throughout aim to be as self contained as possible.
