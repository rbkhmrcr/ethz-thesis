\section{Introduction}\label{sec:tsps-introduction}

Structure-preserving signature schemes have proved useful across a range of privacy-preserving applications, including blind
signatures, delegatable anonymous credentials, and group signatures, to name a
few~\cite{AFRICACRYPT:FucVer10,CCS:CamDriDub17,RSA:CriLys19,PoPETS:MSBM23,AC:Groth06,AC:Groth07,C:LibPetYun15}.

These applications rely on signing group elements directly. A signer can sign a
commitment without opening it, and the holder of the signature can later prove
statements about the committed value in zero knowledge without revealing it. An
ordinary signature scheme hashes the message first, so proving knowledge of a
signature on a commitment needs a general-purpose proof over the hash function.
Signing the group elements directly keeps these proofs algebraic and efficient.

Many systems have an entity that certifies state for everyone else, such as a
transparency log provider or a ledger administrator. In threshold certification,
each signer holds a share of the signing key, and a threshold of them is needed
to sign a commitment, so no single signer or compromised key share can certify
alone. In a multi-signature scheme, by contrast, each signer has its own key
pair, and a verifier checks one combined signature against the public keys of
all signers. With threshold certification, verifiers see one public key and
ordinary signatures.

For a threshold signer, the cost is interaction whenever a signature is needed.
In our schemes, the online phase needs no interaction between signers.

Ledgers are the first setting where such certifiers appear. A ledger records
commitments to tokens or accounts, and to spend a token or report on an account,
its owner needs a signature showing that the commitment is
recorded~\cite{EPRINT:ACDDET19,EPRINT:TBAAGP22,CCS:KiaKohSar22,RSA:PoiSan18,NDSS:SABMD19}.
With an SPS scheme, the certifier signs the committed group elements as they
appear on the ledger and publishes the signatures, without interacting with the
owner, and the owner later proves statements about the committed values in zero
knowledge. Where a ledger is run by several authorities, for example a central
bank digital currency in which every transaction is endorsed before it is
recorded, a threshold signature scheme divides the certification between them.

Transparency logs are the second. Certificate transparency (CT) and key
transparency (KT) logs are append-only, publicly verifiable records of
certificates or keys. Each entry is sent to a log provider, signed, and appended
to the log. In CT the log server returns a signed certificate timestamp to the
certificate provider, and in KT the log server signs the mapping from users'
identities to their public keys. Transparency logs move trust from the application
serving a key onto the log providers, and a threshold signature scheme narrows
what any one of them can do. Signatures from a threshold SPS scheme, given to the
owners of keys, would also let them prove, in zero knowledge, that their keys are
recorded in the log without revealing which keys they are. Since the key is signed
directly, without hashing it first, that proof is efficient.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We give two threshold SPS schemes, building on two
existing SPS schemes together with secure multiparty computation (MPC)
techniques~\cite{C:AGHO11,C:KilPanWee15,C:DPSZ12,CCS:KelOrsSch16,C:GLOPS21}.
We select the SPS scheme of Abe, Groth, Haralambiev and Ohkubo, $\AGHO$, for its
optimal signature size, and that of Kiltz, Pan and Wee, $\KPW$, for its reliance
on standard assumptions alone~\cite{C:AGHO11,C:KilPanWee15}.
Threshold $\AGHO$ outputs ordinary $\AGHO$ signatures of three group elements,
which meets the lower bound for SPS in type III groups, so it has the smallest
possible signatures of any threshold SPS for general messages, but is secure only
in the GGM~\cite{C:AGHO11}.
Existing threshold SPS schemes either need seven group elements, or have smaller
signatures but only sign messages given in both source groups with the same
discrete logarithm, where the bound does not apply~\cite{PKC:MMSST24,AC:CKPSS23}.

Our construction applies to any SPS scheme with decomposable signing, a
property of the signing algorithm that we define, and that $\AGHO$ and $\KPW$
have. The signing key is secret-shared between the signers, and key generation uses
general-purpose MPC. Evaluating signing under MPC directly
would mean the signers interact for each signature. In the SPS schemes we use,
the message is a vector of public group elements raised to shared exponents,
which each signer can do locally. So we do all message-independent computations
under MPC in an offline phase, and once the message is known,
signing needs no interaction. High-throughput applications, such as payments,
have quiet and busy periods, and the offline phase can run in the quiet ones.
\Cref{sec:tsps-security} shows that security
reduces to the unforgeability of the underlying scheme.

One existing threshold SPS scheme avoids interaction altogether, but only signs
pairs of group elements, one in each source group, with the same discrete
logarithm. Existing commitments and public keys lie in one group and cannot be
signed with it, which rules out the ledger and transparency-log uses
above~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,EPRINT:GGPR25}. Splitting signing in two keeps existing
SPS schemes as they are, including $\AGHO$, which has the smallest possible
signatures.

Our second contribution is an implementation. Threshold $\KPW$ needs
secret-shared group elements, and raising a shared group element to a shared
exponent. Computation on shared group elements has been implemented for two
parties, including in pairing groups~\cite{NDSS:GPVRNC23,LC:ADEO21}, and for
many parties in MP-SPDZ on a curve without a pairing~\cite{ESORICS:DOKSS20}. We
add sharings of pairing-group elements and this operation to MP-SPDZ, for any
number of parties~\cite{CCS:Keller20}. To our knowledge, this is the first multiparty
implementation of computation on shared elements of pairing groups.

We benchmark both schemes using two MPC frameworks: ATLAS, which assumes an
honest majority of participants, and MASCOT, which assumes a dishonest
majority~\cite{C:GLOPS21,CCS:KelOrsSch16}. We also
implement previously suggested threshold SPS schemes, so as to compare concrete
efficiency~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,PKC:MMSST24}.

This chapter is about unforgeability and does not treat privacy directly. In
the applications above, privacy comes from composition. The signers sign
committed state, and the holder of a signature discloses what they choose through
a zero-knowledge proof.

\subsection*{Related work}

\paragraph{SPS schemes.} The first SPS scheme signs messages of arbitrary length
with signatures of seven group elements, and is secure in the standard model
under a non-interactive assumption~\cite{C:AFGHO10}. A signature in type III groups
needs at least three group elements, and there are matching constructions in the
GGM, among them the rerandomisable scheme $\AGHO$ that we use~\cite{C:AGHO11}.
SPS schemes with such short signatures cannot be proven secure from non-interactive assumptions
by a large class of reductions~\cite{AC:AbeGroOhk11}. The scheme $\KPW$ has
signatures of seven group elements and is secure under SXDH~\cite{C:KilPanWee15}.
Fully structure-preserving
signature schemes also have secret keys that are group elements, so
a user can prove statements about their own key with Groth-Sahai proofs, which
prove pairing product equations in zero knowledge, but their signatures grow with the square root of the message
length~\cite{JC:AGKOT19,EC:GroSah08}.

\paragraph{Threshold SPS schemes.} Threshold SPS schemes have only recently
been considered. One non-interactive construction is restricted to signing
indexed Diffie-Hellman tuples, of the form $(\gen^\msg,
\ggen^\msg)$~\cite{AC:CKPSS23}.


Following our work, a non-interactive threshold SPS scheme was given that signs
arbitrary group elements and is secure under SXDH~\cite{PKC:MMSST24}. It builds
on $\KPW$, and its signatures consist of seven group elements, against three for
threshold $\AGHO$. We benchmark against it in \Cref{sec:tsps-evaluation}. Building on this scheme, a threshold SPS
scheme with silent setup lets signers generate their keys independently, with no
interactive key generation, at the cost of larger signatures, a trusted
setup and security in the GGM~\cite{EPRINT:GGPR25}.

\Cref{tab:tsps-comp} in \Cref{sec:tsps-evaluation} compares our schemes with the
two prior threshold SPS schemes in terms of assumptions, key and signature size,
and verification cost~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,PKC:MMSST24}.

\paragraph{Computing on shared group elements.} Smart and Talibi Alaoui give
maliciously secure computation over an elliptic curve group, by mapping
multiplication triples over $\Zp$ locally into triples over the
group~\cite{IMA:SmaTal19}. We realise $\algo{ExpGrp}$ with these triples. Linear
secret sharing isomorphisms generalise this approach, and give
Pointcheval-Sanders signatures, short randomisable signatures on field elements,
in MPC, implemented for two
parties~\cite{LC:ADEO21}.
