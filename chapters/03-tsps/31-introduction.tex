\section{Introduction}\label{sec:tsps-introduction}

Structure-preserving signature schemes have proved useful across a range of privacy-preserving applications, including blind
signatures, delegatable anonymous credentials, and group signatures, to name a
few~\cite{AFRICACRYPT:FucVer10,CCS:CamDriDub17,RSA:CriLys19,PoPETS:MSBM23,AC:Groth06,AC:Groth07,C:LibPetYun15}.

\begin{outline}
  \item so a signer can sign a commitment it cannot open, and the holder later
proves statements about the committed value in zero knowledge without revealing it
  \item contrast: an ordinary signature hashes the message first, so proving
knowledge of a signature on a commitment needs a general-purpose proof over the
hash; signing the group elements directly keeps the proofs algebraic and cheap
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item many systems have an entity that certifies state for everyone else:
transparency log provider, ledger administrator or endorser
  \item the role can't be removed (something has to attest that a key was
recorded, a transaction was valid, an account held a balance at a time), but the
capability attached to it can be narrowed
  \item threshold signatures: key shared between signers, a threshold of them
needed to sign; no single signer certifies alone, no single key compromise
forges a certification
  \item why not multi-signatures: they need every signer and verifiers must know
who signed; a threshold scheme keeps one public key and ordinary signatures, so
verifiers don't change
  \item SPS separates verification from knowledge: the signers know the values
they certify, but the signature is on a commitment, so anyone can check its
validity on the ledger without learning the value\rebekah{describe this better:
authority stays with knowledge (the signers see the transaction), only
verification of validity is separated from it}
  \item practicality (as in the rest of the thesis): for a threshold signer the
cost is interaction when a signature is needed; our online phase needs no
interaction between signers
\end{outline}

Ledgers are the first setting where such certifiers appear. A ledger records
commitments to tokens or accounts, and to spend a token or report on an account,
its owner needs a signature showing that the commitment is
recorded~\cite{EPRINT:ACDDET19,EPRINT:TBAAGP22,CCS:KiaKohSar22,RSA:PoiSan18,NDSS:SABMD19}.
With an SPS scheme, the certifier signs the committed group elements as they
appear on the ledger and publishes the signatures, without interacting with the
owner, and the owner later proves statements about the committed values in zero
knowledge. Where a ledger is run by several authorities, for example a central
bank digital currency in which every transaction is endorsed before it is
recorded, a threshold signature scheme divides the certification between them.

Transparency logs are the second. Certificate transparency (CT) and key
transparency (KT) logs are append-only, publicly verifiable records of
certificates or keys. Each entry is sent to a log provider, signed, and appended
to the log. In CT the log server returns a signed certificate timestamp to the
certificate provider, and in KT the log server signs the mapping from users'
identities to their public keys. Transparency logs move trust from the application
serving a key onto the log providers, and a threshold signature scheme narrows
what any one of them can do. Signatures from a threshold SPS scheme, given to the
owners of keys, would also let them prove, in zero knowledge, that their keys are
recorded in the log without revealing which keys they are. Since the key is signed
directly, without hashing it first, that proof is efficient.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We give two threshold SPS schemes, building on two
existing SPS schemes together with secure multiparty computation (MPC)
techniques~\cite{C:AGHO11,C:KilPanWee15,C:DPSZ12,CCS:KelOrsSch16,C:GLOPS21}.
We select $\AGHO$ for its optimal signature size, and $\KPW$ for its reliance
on standard assumptions alone~\cite{C:AGHO11,C:KilPanWee15}.
Threshold $\AGHO$ outputs ordinary $\AGHO$ signatures of three group elements,
which meets the lower bound for SPS in type III groups, so it has the smallest
possible signatures of any threshold SPS for general messages~\cite{C:AGHO11}.
Existing threshold SPS schemes either need seven group elements, or have smaller
signatures but only sign messages given in both source groups with the same
discrete logarithm, where the bound does not apply~\cite{PKC:MMSST24,AC:CKPSS23}.
The price of this is security in the GGM.

\begin{outline}
  \item signing key held in secret-shared form (enough shares determine it, fewer
reveal nothing); key generation, combining and verification then done with
general-purpose MPC, nothing new needed
  \item the hard part is signing: evaluating it under MPC directly means the
signers must interact for each signature
  \item observation: in SPS the message is public group elements and (in the
schemes we use) only gets raised to shared exponents, which each signer can do on
its own shares; if everything else is prepared in advance, no interaction needed at
message time
  \item why not remove interaction completely: that means designing a new scheme
for the threshold setting, as existing threshold SPS schemes do, with their
restrictions on messages or assumptions; splitting signing in two keeps existing
SPS schemes as they are and moves the interaction to before the message is known
\end{outline}

High-throughput applications, such as payments, have quiet and busy periods,
and the preprocessing can run in the quiet ones. \Cref{sec:tsps-security} shows that the
threshold schemes are unforgeable whenever the underlying SPS scheme is.

We call the property of the signing algorithm that makes this possible
decomposable signing, and show that $\AGHO$ and $\KPW$ have it.

\begin{outline}
  \item second contribution: implementation; thresholdising $\KPW$ needs
secret-shared group elements and raising a shared group element to a shared
exponent
  \item Ghosh et al.\ compute on shared group elements between two parties (cite
NDSS:GPVRNC23); as far as we know no multiparty version had been implemented
  \item Dalskov et al.\ implemented threshold ECDSA for many parties in MP-SPDZ
with shared curve points (ESORICS:DOKSS20, cited in the old evaluation); what is
new here is pairing-friendly groups and a shared group element raised to a
shared exponent (your answer: sounds correct but check)
\end{outline}

We benchmark both schemes using two MPC frameworks: ATLAS, which assumes an
honest majority, and MASCOT, which assumes a dishonest
majority~\cite{C:GLOPS21,CCS:KelOrsSch16}. We extend MP-SPDZ with support for
the group element functionalities our schemes require~\cite{CCS:Keller20}. The
former suits settings where robustness and high availability are desirable, the
latter settings where resilience to key compromise takes precedence. We also
implement previously suggested threshold SPS schemes, so as to compare concrete
efficiency~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,PKC:MMSST24}.

This chapter is about unforgeability and does not treat privacy directly. In
the applications above, privacy comes from composition. The signers sign
committed state, and the holder of a signature discloses what they choose through
a zero-knowledge proof.

\subsection*{Related work}

\paragraph{SPS schemes.} The first SPS scheme signs messages of arbitrary length
with signatures of seven group elements, and is secure in the standard model
under a non-interactive assumption~\cite{C:AFGHO10}. Abe, Groth, Haralambiev, and
Ohkubo then showed that a signature in type III groups needs at least three group
elements, and gave matching constructions in the GGM, among them the
rerandomisable scheme $\AGHO$ that we use~\cite{C:AGHO11}.
SPS schemes with such short signatures cannot be proven secure from non-interactive assumptions
by a large class of reductions~\cite{AC:AbeGroOhk11}. Kiltz, Pan, and Wee give a
scheme with seven group elements that is secure under SXDH~\cite{C:KilPanWee15}.
A linearly homomorphic SPS scheme, also with signatures of three group
elements, enables further applications~\cite{C:LPJY13}. Fully structure-preserving
signature schemes also have secret keys that are group elements, so
a user can prove statements about their own key with Groth-Sahai proofs, but
their signatures grow with the square root of the message
length~\cite{JC:AGKOT19,EC:GroSah08}.

\paragraph{Threshold SPS schemes.} Threshold SPS schemes have only recently
been considered. One non-interactive construction is restricted to signing
indexed Diffie-Hellman tuples, of the form $(\gen^\msg,
\ggen^\msg)$~\cite{AC:CKPSS23}.

\begin{outline}
  \item an element that only exists in $\group{1}$ (a key or commitment
already on a ledger) can't be signed unless its owner both knows its discrete
logarithm and provides the corresponding element of $\group{2}$
\end{outline}

Another non-interactive threshold SPS scheme instead is capable of signing
arbitrary group elements, and is secure under the SXDH
assumption~\cite{PKC:MMSST24}. It builds on $\KPW$, and its signatures consist
of seven group elements~\cite{C:KilPanWee15}.

\Cref{tab:tsps-comp} in \Cref{sec:tsps-evaluation} compares our schemes with the
two prior threshold SPS schemes in terms of assumptions, key and signature size,
and verification cost~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,PKC:MMSST24}.

\paragraph{Computing on shared group elements.} Moving a sharing of a field
element into a group without interaction, where each party raises a public
generator to its share, goes back to threshold cryptosystems~\cite{C:DesFra89}.
\begin{outline}
  \item Smart and Talibi Alaoui: maliciously secure computation over an elliptic
curve group, maps multiplication triples over $\Zp$ locally into triples over the
group (cite IMA:SmaTal19)
  \item Aranha et al.: generalise this as linear secret sharing isomorphisms, use
it to realise Pointcheval-Sanders in MPC (cite LC:ADEO21)
  \item we extend to $n$ parties the approach of Ghosh et al., who share group
elements between two parties and compute on the shares (cite NDSS:GPVRNC23)
\end{outline}
