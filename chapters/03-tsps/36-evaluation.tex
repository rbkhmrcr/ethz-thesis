Our implementation builds on MP-SPDZ, a framework for implementing MPC with
preprocessing~\cite{CCS:Keller20}. MP-SPDZ is built for MPC consisting of
exclusively elements of and operations in $\Zp$. For
the protocols of this chapter, we also need operations between elements
of $\group{1}$ and $\group{2}$. We benchmark both the honest-majority setting,
$t < n/2$, and the dishonest-majority setting, $t < n$, using ATLAS and MASCOT
respectively~\cite{C:GLOPS21,CCS:KelOrsSch16}. ATLAS uses Shamir's secret sharing, and
MASCOT makes its multiplication triples by oblivious transfer. Any MPC protocol
with the interfaces of our schemes can be used. Neither protocol is robust, as
both are only secure with abort against malicious signers, and neither is
proven secure against adaptive corruption, so \Cref{lem:tsps-adaptive} does not
apply to them.

Our theorems assume static corruption and an MPC protocol that is secure with
abort against malicious signers. MASCOT provides this with a dishonest majority,
so the theorems cover the MASCOT rows. The ATLAS protocol provides it with an
honest majority, but its implementation in MP-SPDZ is only secure against
honest but curious signers~\cite{CCS:Keller20}. So the ATLAS rows show the cost
of the honest-majority setting, but are not covered by our theorems.

We add to MP-SPDZ sharings of group elements with their authentication
information, and $\algo{ExpGrp}$ with mixed triples. The groups are those of
BLS12-381, implemented with RELIC~\cite{relictoolkit}. As baselines, we implement the centralised $\AGHO$ and
$\KPW$ schemes and two existing threshold SPS schemes in Go with gnark-crypto, with a trusted dealer for the threshold
schemes~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,PKC:MMSST24,gnark-crypto}. The tables call these two
schemes CKPSS23 and MMSST24.

All schemes sign vectors of $\ell = 2$ elements of $\group{1}$. ATLAS runs with
$n = 2t+1$ and MASCOT with $t = n-1$, and the tables are at $n = 5$. Each party
runs in one thread, and all parties run on one MacBook Air with an Apple M2 and
16~GB of RAM, over authenticated channels on that machine. Each timed call starts
from empty preprocessing, so the times of the offline phase and of key
generation include making their triples. We report the median of 100
repetitions after 10 warm-up calls, for party $p_1$. In the tables, the
interquartile range is within 5\% of the median for all but five measurements,
and within 16\% for all.
\Cref{tab:tsps-comp} compares the parameters of the schemes, and
\Cref{tab:tsps-benchmarks,tab:tsps-comms}
give their times, communication and sizes.

The online phase sends nothing in any of the four settings, and its time is
comparable to centralised signing. All interaction is in the offline phase and
in key generation, and there MASCOT is far more expensive than ATLAS. Batching
lowers the offline time per signature. \Cref{fig:tsps-n-times,fig:tsps-n-comm} show time and communication
against the number of parties. Communication grows linearly in $n$, and the
online phase stays below 3~ms up to eleven parties.

The threshold SPS schemes CKPSS23 and MMSST24 have no offline phase. Each signer sends one signature share of 96 or 384 bytes to the
combiner.
\Cref{fig:tsps-baselines} compares them against the number of parties. Our
schemes move the extra cost to before the message is known.
Compared with MMSST24, threshold $\AGHO$ has signatures
of three group elements instead of seven, and verifies in 2.93~ms instead of
4.18~ms, but is secure in the GGM rather than under SXDH~\cite{PKC:MMSST24}.

CKPSS23 has smaller signatures, but each signed element needs a counterpart in
the second group, which on BLS12-381 triples the message size~\cite{AC:CKPSS23}.
