Our implementation builds on MP-SPDZ, a framework for implementing MPC with
preprocessing~\cite{CCS:Keller20}. MP-SPDZ is built for the paradigm of sharing
elements of $\Zp$, with all operations defined between elements of $\Zp$. For
the protocols of this chapter, we also need operations between elements
of $\group{1}$ and $\group{2}$. We benchmark both the honest-majority and
dishonest-majority settings, using ATLAS and MASCOT respectively to realise
multiplication of shared secrets~\cite{CCS:KelOrsSch16,C:GLOPS21}. ATLAS is
designed to work in both the honest but curious and malicious settings, but the
implementation available in MP-SPDZ captures only the honest but curious
case~\cite{CCS:Keller20}. Our theorems assume an MPC protocol secure against
malicious signers, so they do not cover the ATLAS rows.

We add to MP-SPDZ sharings of group elements with their authentication
information, and $\algo{ExpGrp}$ with mixed triples. The groups are those of
BLS12-381, implemented with RELIC~\cite{relictoolkit}. ATLAS uses Shamir sharing,
and MASCOT makes its triples by oblivious transfer. Any MPC protocol with the
interfaces of our schemes would do, and we take one for each corruption setting,
ATLAS where high availability matters and MASCOT where resilience to key
compromise matters. As baselines, we implement the centralised $\AGHO$ and
$\KPW$ schemes and two existing threshold SPS schemes in Go with gnark-crypto, with a trusted dealer for the threshold
schemes~\cite{AC:CKPSS23,PKC:MMSST24,gnark-crypto}. The tables call these two
schemes CKPSS23 and MMSST24.

All schemes sign vectors of $\ell = 2$ elements of $\group{1}$. ATLAS runs with
$n = 2t+1$ and MASCOT with $t = n-1$, and the tables are at $n = 5$. Each party
runs in one thread, and all parties run on one MacBook Air with an Apple M2 and
16~GB of RAM, over authenticated channels on that machine. Each timed call starts
from empty preprocessing, so the times of the offline phase and of key
generation include making their triples. We report the median of 100
repetitions after 10 warm-up calls, for party $p_0$. In the tables, the
interquartile range is within 5\% of the median for all but five measurements,
and within 16\% for all.
\Cref{tab:tsps-comp} compares the parameters of the schemes, and
\Cref{tab:tsps-benchmarks,tab:tsps-comms}
give their times, communication and sizes.

The online phase sends nothing in any of the four settings, and takes between
0.36 and 1.84~ms. This is comparable to centralised signing, which takes 0.84~ms
for $\AGHO$ and 2.00~ms for $\KPW$. All interaction is in the offline phase and
in key generation, and there MASCOT is far more expensive than ATLAS. The offline
phase of threshold $\AGHO$ takes 31.0~ms with MASCOT and 2.09~ms with ATLAS, and
sends over a megabyte per party with MASCOT and half a kilobyte with ATLAS.
Batching 16 states brings this to 8.2~ms per signature under MASCOT and 1.3~ms
under ATLAS. \Cref{fig:tsps-n-times,fig:tsps-n-comm} show time and communication
against the number of parties. Communication grows linearly in $n$, and the
online phase stays below 3~ms up to eleven parties.

The threshold SPS schemes CKPSS23 and MMSST24 have no offline phase. Each signer sends one signature share of 96 or 384 bytes to the
combiner. Computing a signature share takes 2.14~ms in CKPSS23 and 1.26~ms in
MMSST24, and the online phase of threshold
$\AGHO$ and threshold $\KPW$ with MASCOT takes 0.72~ms and 1.84~ms.
\Cref{fig:tsps-baselines} compares them against the number of parties. Our
schemes move the extra cost to before the message is known.
Compared with MMSST24, threshold $\AGHO$ has signatures
of three group elements instead of seven, and verifies in 2.93~ms instead of
4.18~ms, but is secure in the GGM rather than under SXDH~\cite{PKC:MMSST24}.

\begin{outline}
  \item Crites et al.\ only sign messages given in both source groups with the
same discrete logarithm, so can't sign a commitment or public key whose discrete
logarithm nobody knows (cite AC:CKPSS23)
  \item keys, signatures and verification of threshold $\KPW$ are those of
$\KPW$, so an existing $\KPW$ deployment can distribute its signer without
changing any verifier
\end{outline}
