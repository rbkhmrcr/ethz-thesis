\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:txid-evaluation}

We implement both instantiations in Go over the BLS12-381 curve. They use
gnark-crypto v0.21.0 for the groups and the pairing, and gnark v0.16.3 for the
zk-SNARK circuits~\cite{gnark-crypto}. We take all measurements on a MacBook
Air with an Apple M2 and 16\,GB of RAM. Each measurement is 10 warm-up calls
followed by 100 timed repetitions, and we report the median. The interquartile
range is typically within 2\% of the median and at most 8\%, except for
hash-based tracing at small $\ell$, which takes well under a millisecond and
varies by up to 20\%.

We implement the algebraic instantiation as \Cref{prot:txid}. The
hash-based instantiation uses MiMC over the scalar field of BLS12-381, and
gnark's implementation of the zk-SNARK of Groth with its commitment
extension.

We report results at $\ell = 8$, just before algebraic proving steps up, so that each user can submit up to $\Max = 256$
transactions per epoch. \Cref{tab:txid-protocol} gives the time of every
algorithm and the size of the message it sends, and \Cref{tab:txid-relations}
the time of every proof. \Cref{fig:txid-ell-times,fig:txid-ell-sizes} show how
times and sizes grow with $\ell$.

Of the 5\,160 bytes of an algebraic transaction, the tag takes 576, the
commitment 48, the proof of $\rel_6'$ 544, the proof of $\rel_7'$ 3\,312, the
largest part, and the range proof 640. Of the 1\,116 bytes of a hash-based transaction, the tag takes
32, the commitment 48, the proof of $\rel_6$ 512, and the proof of $\rel_7$
with its linking proof 292 and 192. The public tracing key is 24\,624 bytes in
the algebraic instantiation and 48 in the hash-based one.

A proof of the zk-SNARK of Groth with its commitment takes 292 bytes, rather
than the 192 bytes of three group elements. The linking proof adds 160 bytes at
a grant and 192 bytes in a transaction. Tracing is $\Max$ evaluations of $\HT$ instead of
$\Max$ pairings. The two circuits have 668 and 1\,343 constraints, and their
setups take 95.3\,ms and 165\,ms.

The hash-based instantiation gives up the transparent setup, and its prover is
much slower. Which instantiation is preferable depends on whether a
deployment can tolerate a trusted setup, and on whether proving or verification
cost matters more.

Verification time hardly depends on $\ell$. In the hash-based instantiation it
stays at $4.44\,\mathrm{ms}$ for every $\ell$ from $1$ to $12$. In the algebraic
one it grows from $6.55\,\mathrm{ms}$ at $\ell = 1$ to $6.76\,\mathrm{ms}$ at
$\ell = 12$, since the Bulletproofs verifier is linear in $\ell$. The hash-based transaction has
constant size, and the algebraic one grows only with the logarithmic size of the
range proof. Algebraic
proving steps up just after each power of two, since the range proof pads to the
next power of two. In the algebraic instantiation the first grant grows with
$\Max = 2^\ell$, and in both instantiations tracing does. Tracing one user for
one epoch never depends on the size of the ledger or the number of users.

We compare against Hyperledger Idemix, which is based on Camenisch-Lysyanskaya
anonymous credentials~\cite{CCS:CamVan02}. It runs over the same library.
\Cref{tab:txid-idemix} compares a transaction with an Idemix signature, with and
without audit pseudonyms.

Idemix audits by testing one ledger entry at a time, at $0.189\,\mathrm{ms}$ per
entry on one core. Our tracing breaks even with Idemix at
about 200 entries per epoch in the algebraic instantiation and 6 in the
hash-based one. At $10^6$ entries the algebraic instantiation takes
$36.9\,\mathrm{ms}$ against $189\,\mathrm{s}$ for Idemix.
