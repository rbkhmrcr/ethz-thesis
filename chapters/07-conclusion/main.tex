\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis shows that institutions can play central roles in privacy-preserving
protocols without being trusted with users' private information. Issuers,
auditors, service providers and central banks provide availability and
accountability, and are often constrained by legal frameworks. Rather than
removing them from our protocols, we model the roles they already play and
constrain with cryptography what each one can learn and do. The result is
protocols that maintain or improve user privacy, while being cheaper for users
to run and more likely to be adopted.

Decentralisation is often used as a stand-in for privacy, so that any central
party looks like a threat to it. But a decentralised system can reveal
everything, and a central party can learn nothing. When
credential issuance is distributed across many issuers, every issuer can learn
every attribute. In \Cref{chap:agg-creds}, a single registration authority that
learns nothing about attributes makes aggregation cheap and keeps issuers
independent. \Cref{chap:offline-payments} makes the same point from the other
direction. Offline payments need availability, and a secure element provides it
with no server online at all. We should separate the properties we want, such as privacy, availability and accountability, from the architectures
we often reach for to achieve them.

Each chapter starts from a role that exists in practice: certifiers in
\Cref{chap:tsps}, issuers and registration authorities in
\Cref{chap:agg-creds}, auditors in \Cref{chap:txid}, and the central bank,
intermediaries and secure elements in \Cref{chap:offline-payments}. In each case the
institution keeps its function without being trusted with more than that
function requires. Threshold certifiers sign committed values, and no single
one of them can certify alone. The registration authority binds a key to a tag
and learns nothing else, while issuers stay independent and keep no state about
users. The auditor of \Cref{chap:txid} computes tags for one user and one
epoch, and learns nothing about anyone else.

The capabilities our constructions grant are as narrow as each role allows, and
the constructions avoid interaction wherever possible. Threshold signing moves all interaction to an offline phase, issuers of aggregate
credentials never coordinate, users submit transactions for tracing
concurrently without counters, and offline payments need no network at all.
We implement and benchmark every construction.

Each chapter gives a generic construction from standard primitives, and then an
instantiation tailored to Sigma protocols and pairing product equations. These
instantiations often need stronger assumptions, the GGM for $\AGHO$ and $q$-type
assumptions for BBS+ and the Dodis-Yampolskiy PRF.
Instantiations under standard assumptions with comparable efficiency remain
open.

Our security proofs are in the standalone model, since extraction rewinds, and
composable guarantees would need different proof techniques. The chapters share
a credential layer: a registration authority with BBS+ or SPS credentials. A
system that combines credentials, tracing and offline payments would need a
joint security analysis.
