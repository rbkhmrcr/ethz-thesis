\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:conclusion}

\begin{outline}
  \item restate the thesis: practical privacy comes from modelling the participants and responsibilities that systems already have, and constraining with cryptography what each one can learn and do, not from removing them
  \item each chapter starts from a role that exists in practice: certifiers (\Cref{chap:tsps}), issuers and the registration authority (\Cref{chap:agg-creds}), auditors (\Cref{chap:txid}), the central bank, intermediaries and secure elements (\Cref{chap:offline-payments})
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item throughline: authority without knowledge; institutions perform a function without being trusted with users' private information
  \item threshold SPS: certifiers sign committed values and no one certifies alone
  \item aggregate credentials: the registration authority binds a key to a tag and learns nothing else; issuers stay independent and stateless
  \item tracing: the auditor computes tags for one user and one epoch, and learns nothing about anyone else
  \item offline payments: the secure element enforces single use without seeing payments; the auditor learns an identity only from a double spend
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item throughline: scoped capabilities rather than all-or-nothing access
  \item a capability is defined by its owner, its period and its trigger: one user and one epoch in \Cref{chap:txid}, one double-spending event in \Cref{chap:offline-payments}, a disclosed subset of attributes in \Cref{chap:agg-creds}
  \item the design question in each case: what is the smallest capability that meets the need, and is its use visible to the person concerned
  \item link back to the financial privacy argument of \Cref{chap:introduction}: money is not end-to-end encryption, and the realistic alternative is the status quo, banks seeing everything
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item throughline: practicality as a design constraint
  \item less interaction: offline signing phase in \Cref{chap:tsps}, no issuer coordination in \Cref{chap:agg-creds}, concurrent submission without counters in \Cref{chap:txid}, payments with no network in \Cref{chap:offline-payments}
  \item costs that do not grow with the system: presentation in $k$ not $n$, tracing in $\Max$ not the size of the ledger, secure element work about one signature
  \item every construction implemented and measured; headline numbers per chapter
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item throughline: generic constructions with tailored instantiations
  \item each chapter gives a generic protocol from standard primitives, then an instantiation built for Sigma protocols and pairing equations; the cost is often stronger assumptions (GGM through AGHO, $q$-type assumptions for BBS+ and Dodis-Yampolskiy)
  \item open: instantiations under standard assumptions with comparable efficiency
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item open questions across chapters
  \item composability: proofs are standalone because extraction rewinds; straight-line extraction would give composable results at the cost of larger proofs
  \item the chapters share a credential layer (registration authority, BBS+ or SPS credentials); a joint analysis of one system combining credentials, tracing and offline payments
  \item threshold auditors and certifiers: the techniques of \Cref{chap:tsps} could split the auditor of \Cref{chap:txid} and \Cref{chap:offline-payments} between institutions
  \item deployment questions that are not only cryptographic: choosing $\Max$, revocation policies, who may compel a grant
\end{outline}
