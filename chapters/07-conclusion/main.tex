\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis argued that practical privacy comes from modelling the participants
and responsibilities that systems already have, and constraining with
cryptography what each one can learn and do, rather than from removing them.
Each chapter started from a role that exists in practice: certifiers in
\Cref{chap:tsps}, issuers and the registration authority in
\Cref{chap:agg-creds}, auditors in \Cref{chap:txid}, and the central bank,
intermediaries and secure elements in \Cref{chap:offline-payments}.

In each case an institution keeps its function without being trusted with more
than that function needs. Threshold certifiers sign committed values, and no
single one of them can certify alone. The registration authority binds a key to
a tag and learns nothing else, while issuers stay independent and keep no state
about users. The auditor of \Cref{chap:txid} computes tags for one user and one
epoch, and learns nothing about anyone else. In offline payments the secure
element enforces single use, and the auditor learns an identity only from a
double spend.

The same pattern appears in the capabilities that the constructions grant. A
capability is scoped by whom it concerns, the period it covers and what triggers
it: one user and one epoch in \Cref{chap:txid}, one double-spending event in
\Cref{chap:offline-payments}, and a disclosed subset of attributes in
\Cref{chap:agg-creds}. The design question is the same in every chapter: what is
the smallest capability that meets the need, and can the person concerned see
when it is used. This brings us back to the argument of
\Cref{chap:introduction}. Money is not end-to-end encryption, and the realistic
alternative to scoped access is the status quo, in which banks see everything.

Practicality was a design constraint throughout. The constructions avoid
interaction where they can. Threshold signing moves all interaction to an
offline phase, issuers of aggregate credentials never coordinate, users submit
transactions for tracing concurrently without counters, and offline payments
need no network at all. Every construction is implemented and measured, so these
claims come with concrete costs.

Each chapter gives a generic construction from standard primitives, and then an
instantiation tailored to Sigma protocols and pairing product equations. The
price of that efficiency is often a stronger assumption: the GGM through
$\AGHO$, and $q$-type assumptions for BBS+ and the Dodis-Yampolskiy PRF.
Instantiations under standard assumptions with comparable efficiency remain
open.

Several questions cut across the chapters. Our security proofs are in the
standalone model, since extraction rewinds, and composable guarantees would need
different proof techniques. The chapters share a credential layer, a
registration authority with BBS+ or SPS credentials, and one system combining
credentials, tracing and offline payments deserves a joint analysis. The
auditor of \Cref{chap:offline-payments} is already split between institutions by
threshold decryption, and the same could be done for the auditor of
\Cref{chap:txid}, while the techniques of \Cref{chap:tsps} could split the
registration authority and the issuers. Finally, some deployment questions are
not only cryptographic, such as choosing the per-epoch transaction bound, setting
revocation policies, and deciding who may compel an auditor to act.
