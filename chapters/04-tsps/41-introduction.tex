\section{Introduction}\label{sec:tsps-introduction}

\begin{outline}
  \item SPS schemes sign vectors of group elements, and verification only checks
pairing-product equations (cite C:AFGHO10, C:AGHO11)
  \item so a signer can sign a commitment it cannot open, and the holder later
proves statements about the committed value in zero knowledge without revealing it
  \item contrast: an ordinary signature hashes the message first, so proving
knowledge of a signature on a commitment needs a general-purpose proof over the
hash; signing the group elements directly keeps the proofs algebraic and cheap
  \item used to build blind signatures, delegatable anonymous credentials, group
signatures, among others (cite AFRICACRYPT:FucVer10, CCS:CamDriDub17,
RSA:CriLys19, PoPETS:MSBM23, AC:Groth06, AC:Groth07, C:LibPetYun15)
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item many systems have an entity that certifies state for everyone else:
transparency log provider, ledger administrator or endorser
  \item the role can't be removed (something has to attest that a key was
recorded, a transaction was valid, an account held a balance at a time), but the
capability attached to it can be narrowed
  \item threshold signatures: key shared between signers, a threshold of them
needed to sign; no single signer certifies alone, no single key compromise
forges a certification
  \item why not multi-signatures: they need every signer and verifiers must know
who signed; a threshold scheme keeps one public key and ordinary signatures, so
verifiers don't change
  \item SPS separates authority from knowledge: signers certify committed values
without learning them
  \item practicality (as in the rest of the thesis): for a threshold signer the
cost that matters is interaction when a signature is needed; our online phase
needs no interaction between signers once the message is known
\end{outline}

Ledgers are the first setting where such certifiers appear. A ledger is a shared, append-only record of state, such as
tokens, accounts or transactions, maintained by one or more authorities. In
ledger-based payment systems, currency is represented by a token encoding its
owner, value, and a unique serial number, and the ledger records a hiding
commitment to the token rather than the token
itself~\cite{EPRINT:ACDDET19,EPRINT:TBAAGP22,CCS:KiaKohSar22,C:Pedersen91}. To
spend a token, its owner needs a signature from a certifier showing that the
commitment is recorded on the ledger, and later proves, via the serial number,
that the token has not been spent before~\cite{RSA:PoiSan18,NDSS:SABMD19}. With
an SPS scheme, the certifier signs the group elements of the
commitment as they appear on the ledger and publishes the signatures, without
interacting with the owner. This is more than an efficiency gain. An interactive
issuance protocol gives the certifier a channel to each spender, which can be
timed, logged and correlated even when the signed value stays hidden, and
publishing signatures over ledger state removes that channel.

The same applies where the ledger stores commitments to accounts, pairs of owner
and holdings, rather than to tokens. Such accounts are the basis for periodic
reporting, for instance proving that holdings do not exceed some threshold when
filing taxes. A tax authority typically has no direct access to the ledger, and
instead relies on signatures produced by the ledger's administrators. With an
SPS scheme, the administrators sign each committed account
together with a timestamp and publish the results, and users prove properties of
their holdings from them without disclosing anything else. Where a ledger is
run by several authorities, for example a central bank digital currency in which
every transaction is endorsed before it is recorded, a threshold signature
scheme divides the endorsement between them.

Transparency logs are the second. Certificate transparency (CT) and key
transparency (KT) logs are append-only, publicly verifiable records of
certificates or keys. Each entry is sent to a log provider, signed, and appended
to the log. In CT the log server returns a signed certificate timestamp to the
certificate provider, and in KT the log server signs the mapping from users'
identities to their public keys. Transparency logs move trust from the application
serving a key onto the log providers, and a threshold signature scheme narrows
what any one of them can do. Signatures from a threshold SPS scheme, given to the
owners of keys, would also let them prove, in zero knowledge, that their keys are
recorded in the log without revealing which keys they are. Since the key is signed
directly rather than hashed first, that proof is efficient.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{outline}
  \item two threshold SPS schemes, built from $\AGHO$ and $\KPW$ (cite C:AGHO11,
C:KilPanWee15); $\AGHO$ for its optimal signature size, $\KPW$ because it is
secure under SXDH rather than in the GGM
  \item signing key held in secret-shared form (enough shares determine it, fewer
reveal nothing); key generation, combining and verification then done with
general-purpose MPC, nothing new needed
  \item the hard part is signing: evaluating it under MPC directly means the
signers interact after the message is known
  \item observation: in SPS the message is public group elements and (in the
schemes we use) only gets raised to secret exponents, which each signer can do on
its own shares; if everything else is prepared in advance, no interaction once a
message arrives
  \item why not remove interaction completely: that means designing a new scheme
for the threshold setting, as existing threshold SPS schemes do, with their
restrictions on messages or assumptions; splitting signing in two keeps existing
SPS schemes as they are and moves the interaction off the critical path
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item first contribution: decomposition of SPS signing into an offline phase
(all interaction, before the message is known) and an online phase each signer
runs locally
  \item we define the property of the signing algorithm that makes this possible
(\Cref{sec:tsps-construction}), show $\AGHO$ and $\KPW$ have it, and prove the
threshold schemes unforgeable whenever the underlying scheme is
  \item suits bursty traffic, e.g.\ payments: states prepared in quiet periods,
used in busy ones
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item second contribution: implementation; thresholdising $\KPW$ needs
secret-shared group elements and multiplication of a shared field element with a
shared group element
  \item Ghosh et al.\ compute on shared group elements between two parties (cite
NDSS:GPVRNC23); as far as we know no multiparty version is implemented
  \item we extend MP-SPDZ with sharings of group elements and this
multiplication (cite CCS:Keller20)
  \item benchmark both schemes with ATLAS (honest majority: fewer than half
corrupted) and MASCOT (dishonest majority: up to all but one corrupted) (cite
C:GLOPS21, CCS:KelOrsSch16); ATLAS where robustness and availability matter,
MASCOT where resilience to key compromise matters
  \item also implement the two existing threshold SPS schemes and compare (cite
AC:CKPSS23, PKC:MMSST24)
\end{outline}

This chapter treats unforgeability rather than privacy directly. Privacy in the
applications above comes from composition: the signers sign committed state, and
the holder of a signature discloses what they choose through a zero-knowledge
proof.

\subsection*{Related work}

\paragraph{SPS schemes.} SPS schemes were introduced by Abe et al., whose
scheme signs messages of arbitrary length
with signatures of seven group elements, and is secure in the standard model
under a non-interactive assumption~\cite{C:AFGHO10}. Abe, Groth, Haralambiev, and
Ohkubo then showed that a signature in type III groups needs at least three group
elements, and gave matching constructions in the GGM, among them the
rerandomisable scheme $\AGHO$ that we use~\cite{C:AGHO11}.
SPS schemes with such short signatures cannot be proven secure from non-interactive assumptions
by a large class of reductions~\cite{AC:AbeGroOhk11}. Kiltz, Pan, and Wee give a
scheme with seven group elements that is secure under SXDH~\cite{C:KilPanWee15}.
A linearly homomorphic SPS scheme, also with signatures of three group
elements, enables further applications~\cite{C:LPJY13}. Fully structure-preserving
signature schemes additionally have secret keys that are group elements, so
a user can prove statements about their own key with Groth-Sahai proofs, a
proof system for pairing-product equations, at the
cost of signatures that grow with the square root of the message
length~\cite{JC:AGKOT19,EC:GroSah08}.

\paragraph{Threshold SPS schemes.}
\begin{outline}
  \item only recently considered
  \item Crites et al.\ (cite AC:CKPSS23): non-interactive, but signs only indexed
Diffie-Hellman pairs, each message given with the same discrete logarithm in both
source groups; an element that only exists in $\group{1}$ (a key or commitment
already on a ledger) can't be signed unless its owner supplies the $\group{2}$
element
  \item Mitrokotsa et al.\ (cite PKC:MMSST24): non-interactive, signs arbitrary
vectors of group elements, built from $\KPW$, SXDH, seven group elements
  \item neither has an offline phase; the evaluation compares both with ours
\end{outline}

\paragraph{Computing on shared group elements.}
\begin{outline}
  \item moving a sharing of a field element into a group without interaction
(each party raises a public generator to its share) goes back to verifiable
secret sharing (cite FOCS:Feldman87)
  \item Smart and Talibi Alaoui: maliciously secure computation over an elliptic
curve group, maps multiplication triples over $\Zp$ locally into triples over the
group (cite IMA:SmaTal19)
  \item Aranha et al.: generalise this as linear secret sharing isomorphisms, use
it to realise Pointcheval-Sanders in MPC (cite LC:ADEO21)
  \item we extend to $n$ parties the approach of Ghosh et al., who share group
elements between two parties and compute on the shares (cite NDSS:GPVRNC23)
\end{outline}
