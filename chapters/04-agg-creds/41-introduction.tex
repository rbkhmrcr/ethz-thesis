\section{Introduction}

An attribute is a value about a user that an issuer certifies, such as their
age, their nationality or a driving licence. Anonymous credential schemes let
users prove they hold certain attributes without revealing who they are or
anything about their undisclosed attribute values. Issuers give users
credentials on their attribute values, and users present these credentials to
verifiers. The two main security properties are unlinkability, so two
presentations of the same user cannot be tied to one another, and
unforgeability, so a user can only successfully attest to having attribute
values that they truly possess~\cite{EC:CamLys01}.

Idemix and U-Prove implement anonymous credentials, Signal uses them for
private groups, and Privacy Pass issues related anonymous
tokens~\cite{CCS:CamVan02,UProve,CCS:ChaPerZav20,PoPETS:DGSTV18}. By the end of
2026, every member state of the European Union must offer a European Digital
Identity Wallet with selective disclosure, unlinkability and pseudonymous
authentication~\cite{eIDAS2024}. Its attributes come from many issuers, such as
a passport agency, a licensing body and a transport authority, each certifying
its own.

Early anonymous credential schemes assume a single issuer certifying all
attributes, creating a single entity responsible for all issuance, and able to
learn all attribute values of every user. Later schemes distribute issuance
across multiple authorities or delegate it to
sub-issuers~\cite{NDSS:SABMD19,SP:DKLsT23,CCS:KRBKKL24}, but the issuer,
though now decentralised, remains conceptually responsible for all attributes.
This reduces trust in any one party, but does not reflect real-world scenarios,
where distinct entities independently certify one or a small subset of
attributes.

Credential systems with distributed issuance still require the issuers to coordinate. With many
issuers worldwide, each relevant to only some users, that coordination is
impractical, and between issuers with no relationship to one another it may be
impossible. Coordination between issuers is the obstacle. Threshold issuance
with silent setup removes interactive setup for issuers certifying the same
credential~\cite{EPRINT:GGPR25}. We remove coordination for issuers certifying
different attributes.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We give an aggregate anonymous credential
scheme: credentials from independent, uncoordinated issuers
combine into one compact credential, provable in one proof. Aggregation is
performed locally by the user without interaction between issuers. The scheme
supports selective disclosure of attribute values.

In our scheme, a credential rests on a chain of
links: from a user's secret key to their public key, from the public key to a
tag, and from the tag to the credentials issued under it. Each link can be
established by a different party. Users show that they know their secret key. A
registration authority, which certifies users' keys in any case, links each
public key to a tag. Issuers then sign attributes under the tag, and their
signatures do not involve the key. At presentation, the verifier checks all three
links together, and so sees that the chain is unbroken without learning the key
or the tag.

The registration authority sees no attribute values and is not involved after
registration. To present
attributes, users aggregate the relevant credentials and prove, in zero
knowledge, that they know the key, hold a valid aggregate credential for the
disclosed values, and that both share a tag. Binding by tag stops
different users' credentials being pooled, and lets honest users aggregate
credentials from issuers that have never communicated, without contacting any
issuer again. Separating the requirements on the registration authority from
those on the issuers also lets issuers be stateless. Each user has one tag, so
issuers need not remember whom they have signed for.

The registration authority is not a new party that we add to the model. Every
anonymous credential scheme already assumes that users' keys are certified, so
that credentials cannot be issued to keys that users do not control. That
assumption is usually left implicit, or met by the issuer itself. Here we name
the role.

\subsection*{Related work}

\paragraph{Single-issuer anonymous credential systems.} An early anonymous
credential system issues signatures on committed values~\cite{EC:CamLys01,SCN:CamLys02}. In a
later pairing-based construction, signature size and verification complexity
grow linearly with the number of attribute values
signed~\cite{C:CamLys04}. An extension supports a constrained form of
aggregation, but only in settings where all values are known in
advance~\cite{FC:LeeLeeYun13}.

Credentials built on the BBS+ signature scheme support selective disclosure,
where a user reveals some of their attribute values and hides the rest, in a
single-issuer setting, with verification linear in the total number of
attributes, and form the basis of the ongoing standardisation effort of the
Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF)~\cite{EC:BonBoy04a,SCN:AuSusMu06}.

Further improvements targeted efficiency. The Pointcheval-Sanders signature
scheme introduces randomisable signatures with sequential aggregation, and
proves unforgeability in the GGM~\cite{RSA:PoiSan16}. More recently, structure-preserving
signatures on equivalence classes, whose signatures also hold for every scalar
multiple of the signed vector, enable efficient proofs, with security proven in the GGM~\cite{JC:FucHanSla19}. Unlinkable
proofs of knowledge over a subset of signed messages, building on the
Pointcheval-Sanders signature scheme, are also proven secure in the
GGM~\cite{PKC:Sanders20}.

\paragraph{Multi-issuer anonymous credential systems.} Multi-issuer schemes
decentralise the issuer, delegating issuance to sub-issuers or distributing issuance
responsibilities among several
issuers~\cite{TCC:BCKL08,CCS:CamDriDub17,NDSS:SABMD19}.

A multi-issuer credential system supports selective disclosure and
pseudonyms~\cite{AC:CDHK15}. It is proven secure in the universal composability
framework, with a common reference string~\cite{FOCS:Canetti01}. Credentials from different
issuers are linked through a pseudonym and not aggregated, so a presentation
contains one proof per credential.

\paragraph{Aggregate anonymous credentials.} Aggregate credentials combine
independently issued credentials into a single compact credential. Indexed
aggregate signature schemes support anonymous credentials this way, and are the
closest prior work to ours~\cite{canard2011anonymous}. Users get an index from a
registration authority, issuers keep no state, and credentials from several
issuers aggregate into one constant-size presentation. Issuers see the index in
the clear, as ours see the tag. The number of users there is bounded by a
$q$-type assumption, issuer keys need no certification, and security is
game-based only. Our scheme has no bound on the number of users, is proven
secure through an ideal functionality, and is implemented.

ART-Sign is a traceable aggregate signature scheme using randomisable tags as
pseudonyms, from which H\'ebant and Pointcheval build multi-authority
credentials~\cite{SCN:HebPoi22}. Presentations are of
constant size, and a designated authority can trace users. The signatures are
linearly homomorphic, so signatures on two messages combine into a signature on
any linear combination of them, and two signatures by one issuer under one tag and index
would let anyone sign any value for that index. Issuers sign at most once per
tag and index, and keep a record of what they have signed for each user. In a
bounded variant they instead count the messages signed per tag, and the issuer
key grows with the bound.

Issuer-hiding multi-authority credentials, where a verifier learns only that
the issuer lies in a set it accepts, can also be built from aggregate
signatures with randomisable tags and keys~\cite{CCS:MBGLS23}. The issuers there
are not stateless either. Either the attributes and issuer keys of a user are fixed
when the user's tag is created, or each issuer signs only once per tag. The authors name the
stateless, dynamic setting as an open question.

\paragraph{Other multi-authority credentials.} Other multi-authority
credentials are in different settings. Threshold issuance without interactive
setup lets uncoordinated issuers jointly certify one
credential, with a constant-size presentation~\cite{EPRINT:GGPR25}. Here, each
issuer certifies its own attributes instead. Independent authorities can also each sign a user's global
identity, with one proof per credential~\cite{anada2019decentralized}.
Credentials can be derived from existing identity documents with zk-SNARKs,
general-purpose zero-knowledge proofs that are short and quick to verify,
without new issuer infrastructure, at the cost of a general-purpose proof per
credential~\cite{SP:RWGM23}. Issuer hiding is an orthogonal problem~\cite{CANS:BEKRS21,PoPETS:SanTra24}. We would like to address it in
future work.

\paragraph{Comparison.} In ART-Sign the tag is the user's public key, whose
validity is proven again at every presentation, while ours is a separate value
that the registration authority signs together with the key, so issuers keep
no record.

included, rely on $q$-type assumptions, interactive assumptions or the GGM,
