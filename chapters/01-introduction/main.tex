\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

The desire to conceal written information has been considered for at least the
last four thousand years, and the obfuscated hieroglyphs in the tomb of
Khnumhotep II are one of the earliest known examples. More recently, encryption
schemes have let people communicate by exchanging messages that are, to
eavesdroppers, indistinguishable from random. Parties with a shared key can send
each other confidential messages, whatever the content of those messages, and
whatever the wealth, affiliations or power of the parties. Modern cryptography
has made encrypted communication that even very powerful entities cannot read.

This is possible because the communicating parties need not be as powerful as
the entities trying to break the system. Encryption requires relatively little
computational power of its users, while the adversary's task remains
computationally infeasible. This asymmetry in the computational power necessary
for users and for attackers lies at the foundation of modern
cryptography~\cite{DifHel76}. However, protecting the content of a message says
nothing about who is exchanging it, when, or how often.

Cryptography today therefore covers much more than private communication. Once
the contents of a communication are protected, a resourceful adversary may
instead try to learn the identities of the communicating parties, their
relationships, or patterns in their behaviour. Protecting each of these brings
the next piece of observable information into focus.

Over the last fifty years, privacy-enhancing technologies have been designed to
address these broader privacy problems. As the surface of protected information
has grown, so has the number of entities capable of observing some part of the
system. Service providers, issuers, validators, network operators and public
authorities may all learn sensitive information while performing otherwise
legitimate functions. These entities are part of the system, and a protocol has
to say what each of them is allowed to learn. The answer depends on how they
are modelled.

Most often, they are modelled as adversaries. Protocol designers have
understandably worked to take capabilities away from large, centralised
institutions, and anything such an institution learns is considered a step away
from the ideal world.

\paragraph{Centralisation, responsibility and trust.} Removing trust from
centralised institutions has a cost. Protocols that do so often transfer
computation, storage and operational responsibility to individual users
instead. Moreover, centralisation is not a single property, and decentralisation
alone is not a complete description of any protocol's design.

Computation, state, authority and information may each be distributed
differently. A system may centralise computation while distributing authority
between several institutions, or centralise storage while using cryptography to
prevent the storage provider from learning the stored information. The
distribution of these responsibilities does not by itself determine the trust
assumptions of the system. An institution may be relied upon to provide
availability, maintain state or execute a prescribed computation without ever
having access to users' private information.

Two cryptographic concepts illustrate this: anonymous credentials and
multiparty computation.

In anonymous credential schemes, issuers issue credentials to users, attesting
that users hold certain attribute values. Users present their credentials,
proving they hold given attributes without revealing who they are or anything
about their undisclosed attribute values. Early anonymous credential schemes
assumed a single issuer certifying all attributes, creating one powerful entity.
Later schemes decentralised issuance, but the issuers as a whole remain
responsible for all attributes, so that all issuers can now learn all users'
attribute values.

\begin{outline}
  \item point to add: decentralising issuance divided the power to certify, but
  not the knowledge that comes with it
\end{outline}

Multiparty computation allows a function to be computed by multiple mutually
distrusting parties, each with a private input, while revealing nothing but the
output. It seems to be a case where decentralising a protocol leads directly to
a privacy gain for users, and it can be applied to credential issuance to
sidestep the problem above. The new problem it introduces, however, is in
practicality. Multiparty computation generally needs every participant to
interact, which becomes infeasible when, for example, a driving licence, a birth
certificate and a passport are each issued by countries with no diplomatic
relations with one another. These issuers have no incentive to take part in an
expensive protocol so that individuals can privately prove statements about
themselves.

\begin{outline}
  \item point to add: MPC issuance still gives every issuer a share of all
  users' attributes, so the data is spread across more institutions, even if
  none can read it alone
\end{outline}

Privacy does not automatically follow from decentralisation, then, nor does it
automatically disappear when corporations or public institutions participate in
a protocol. What matters is the information made available to each participant.
And so practicality is also an aim in all of our constructions. By this, we mean
minimising the interaction, coordination, communication and computational costs
required by the protocols.

\begin{outline}
  \item what matters is also whether the protocol can actually be run by the
  participants that exist
  \item practicality means more than running time: a protocol needing every
  institution online at once, or one institution taking part in every other
  institution's operations, will not be deployed however fast each step is
  \item costs we count: parties that must be online together, rounds between
  them, coordination needed before anything starts, plus communication and
  computation
\end{outline}

\begin{outline}
  \item much of the privacy literature treats any party that learns something
  as a threat to be designed out
  \item often not possible: an issuer, validator or auditor exists because the
  system needs one
  \item removing them does not remove the need; their function goes either to
  users (who bear the cost) or outside the protocol (where nothing limits what
  they learn)
  \item leads into the decentralised privacy vs centralised surveillance binary
\end{outline}

Privacy-focused systems are often discussed as if the choice is between
decentralised privacy and centralised surveillance. This thesis challenges that
binary. Institutions can perform useful protocol functions while cryptography
limits both the information available to them and the capabilities they
receive, and allowing them to do this work may reduce the computation, storage,
time and specialist knowledge required of individual users.

Systems deployed today already work this way. Privacy Pass, anonymous
credential schemes, and privacy services such as Apple's Private Relay use
institutional infrastructure while limiting the information available to the
institutions involved~\cite{PoPETS:DGSTV18,privaterelay2021}. This thesis
explores how modelling such roles explicitly can enable practical and efficient
systems.

\paragraph{Financial privacy.} Cryptographic protocols are applied in vastly
different spaces. There are strong arguments against governments
reading their citizens' communications, and companies their customers'
messages. A private communication system can aim to ensure that no party other
than the intended recipient learns the contents of a message. But in a
financial system, there is something slightly different involved.

\begin{outline}
  \item financial systems differ from messaging, for three reasons
  \item structure: the internet and messaging are decentralised (anyone can run
  a server, no permission needed to talk); money is issued by central banks,
  held by regulated intermediaries, settled through few institutions, and this
  is set by law, not protocol design
  \item socially different: a payment affects parties beyond the two making it,
  and society gives some (tax authorities, courts) a claim to see it
  \item technically different: a token of value must not be spent twice, a
  message can be copied freely
  \item the alternative to private finance is not perfect privacy but the status
  quo, where banks and payment providers see every transaction in the clear
\end{outline}

Real financial systems therefore have legitimate accountability requirements.
Financial systems must establish the
validity of transactions, maintain a consistent shared state, control issuance,
and prevent or detect double spending~\cite{C:Chaum82,C:ChaFiaNao88}. They must
remain efficient as the number of participants grows, ideally without the cost
per participant growing linearly with the size of the system. They must also
continue to function for users with no network connection, for resilience of
the payment system as a whole~\cite{aboulaiz2024offline}. A system that controls
a token of value is more complex than a system dealing only with private
communication of messages.

\begin{outline}
  \item after the first sentence above: AML/CFT rules require payment providers
  to identify customers and report suspicious transactions (needs a citation,
  none in misc.bib yet)
  \item central banks designing digital currencies list privacy alongside these
  requirements, not instead of them (needs a citation)
\end{outline}

This thesis takes the existence of financial auditing requirements as a system
constraint and asks how narrowly the corresponding capabilities can be defined.
What information should an auditor be able to obtain? Under which circumstances
can they obtain it? Should access be limited to a transaction, a user, a
certain period of time, a single prescribed purpose, or be ongoing?

Constructing digital financial systems with auditability may seem analogous to
building backdoors in messaging systems, and there is the argument that once a
capability exists, it will be used everywhere possible. This is important to
consider. Financial systems are inherently responsible for movement of money.
This instantly distinguishes the system from communication alone. Refusing to
design auditing protocols does not remove the accountability requirements
already present in financial systems. It may instead result in the auditing
goals being met by systems offering much weaker privacy to all users.

\begin{outline}
  \item choosing which systems to build is a moral as well as a technical
  choice, cite EPRINT:Rogaway15
  \item here the choice is between auditing that cryptography constrains and
  auditing that nothing constrains
\end{outline}

We therefore start from the operational roles these systems already have, and
ask how protocols can work with them. An institution need not be modelled in the
same way for the analysis of each property of a system. For availability and
correct operation, we assume that institutions perform their prescribed roles.
For privacy, the same institutions are not trusted to keep information available
to them confidential. They may retain and combine this information, share it
with other participants, and attempt to learn anything they can, and privacy is
assessed against the strongest information they can derive in this setting. An
institution may therefore be relied upon to perform an operational function
without being trusted with users' private information. Each chapter states
which participants may deviate from the protocol, and for which property.

\paragraph{This thesis.} We argue that practical privacy is achieved
not by assuming away existing infrastructure, but by modelling its participants
and responsibilities accurately, and cryptographically constraining what each
participant can learn and do. In particular, an institution may perform
computation, maintain state, issue credentials, validate transactions or provide
availability without being trusted with users' private information. Each of the
protocols comes from considering the specific responsibilities of participants
in an existing system.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We first consider aggregate anonymous credentials.
Existing credential models do not always reflect the way that authority is
divided in practice. Users may receive attributes from several independent
issuers and later need to demonstrate statements about subsets of them. We focus
on decentralisation of issuance. We construct an efficient multi-issuer
aggregate anonymous credential scheme in which independently issued credentials
can be combined and presented through a single proof. Anonymous credential
schemes already implicitly include a registration authority. We use this
existing role to bind credentials issued to the same user, preventing
credentials belonging to different users from being aggregated without requiring
expensive proofs during presentation.

\begin{outline}
  \item link back to the decentralised issuance problem in the credentials and
  MPC paragraphs above
  \item insight that set the direction: the difficulty of combining credentials
  from uncoordinated issuers lies less in the cryptography than in where the
  model places trust
  \item the registration authority certifies users' keys; treating it as a role
  in its own right means binding needs no coordination between issuers
\end{outline}

We then construct threshold structure-preserving signature schemes that
require no interaction during the online signing phase, making them suitable for
efficient transaction authorisation or other multi-certifier settings. Here
again, the existing structure becomes a resource rather than an obstacle to be
removed.

\begin{outline}
  \item framing: institutions that certify state
  \item define threshold signatures: signing key shared between several
  signers, a signature needs a threshold number of them
  \item define structure-preserving signatures: sign group elements such as
  commitments directly, so the holder can later prove statements about what was
  signed without revealing it
  \item insight that shaped the design: most of a threshold scheme can be left
  to general-purpose MPC, only signing needs its own treatment
  \item cost of cooperation is paid before the message arrives, no interaction
  once it is known, so an institution can divide its authority to certify
  without slowing down certification
\end{outline}

We next consider accountability in transaction systems that protect user
privacy. Financial systems may require both the ability to investigate a
particular transaction and the ability, under prescribed circumstances, to
identify the transactions associated with a particular user. These capabilities
are related but distinct, and granting one need not grant the other. We develop a transaction
identification protocol that supports concurrent transaction creation without
requiring the auditor to maintain persistent per-user state. Auditing is
restricted to a specified epoch, a fixed period of time, limiting both the scope
and the duration of the information disclosed to an auditor.

\begin{outline}
  \item after ``granting one need not grant the other'': the question behind
  this work was not whether an auditor should exist, but whether its capability
  can be scoped like any other permission (one user, one period, only once
  granted)
\end{outline}

Finally, we study privacy in offline payments. Offline operation improves
availability and accessibility, but changes how double spending must be handled.
We develop an offline payment scheme that divides computation between a mobile
device and a secure element, a small tamper-resistant chip, such as a smart
card, that users carry but cannot easily modify. Payments made by
honest users remain unlinkable, meaning they cannot be tied to one another or to
the payer, while information identifying the double spender can be recovered
following double spending.

\begin{outline}
  \item without a network, something must stop users spending the same value
  twice; the natural candidate is a component institutions already issue (the
  secure element)
  \item the secure element is trusted for one narrow rule, the device does
  everything else
\end{outline}
