\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

The desire to conceal written information has been considered for at least the
last four thousand years, from the obfuscated hieroglyphs found in the tomb of
Khnumhotep II. More recently, encryption schemes allow people to communicate by
exchanging messages that reveal nothing about their content to eavesdroppers.
Modern cryptography has enabled encrypted communication that even truly
powerful entities cannot read, while demanding relatively little computational
power of the users~\cite{DifHel76}.

However, encrypting the content of a message does not hide who is exchanging
it, when, or how often. Once the contents of a communication are
protected, a resourceful adversary may instead try to learn the identities of
the communicating parties, their relationships, or patterns in their behaviour.
Protecting each of these brings the next piece of observable information into
focus.

As the adoption of digital systems has grown, so has the number of
entities capable of observing some part of a system. Service providers,
issuers, validators, network operators and public authorities may all learn
sensitive information while performing otherwise legitimate functions.  This
raises the question of how these participants should be incorporated into the
system model, and what information they should be permitted to learn.

These entities are often modelled as adversaries.  Protocol designers have
understandably been active in designing systems that take power away from
centralised entities. Anything that such an entity learns is considered a step
away from the ideal world.

\paragraph{Blockchains and cryptocurrencies.} A distributed ledger is a record of transactions that
is replicated across many participants, who agree on its contents through a
consensus protocol, so that no single party controls it or has to be trusted to
keep it correct. A blockchain is a distributed ledger in which transactions are
grouped into blocks chained together by hashes. Cryptocurrencies use such a
ledger to record the ownership and transfer of value, replacing the issuer and
the bank with validators who follow the protocol for economic rather than
institutional reasons~\cite{bitcoin,ethereum}. Anyone can read and verify the
ledger, which makes the system available and open to all, but the same
transparency is the main cost to privacy.

In Bitcoin and
Ethereum, users appear on the ledger as keys rather than names, but every
transaction, its amount and its links to other transactions are public, so
users are pseudonymous rather than anonymous. Privacy-focused cryptocurrencies
hide more. Monero hides the sender of a transaction among a set of other users
with ring signatures, and Zcash uses zero-knowledge proofs to hide the sender,
the recipient and the value~\cite{EPRINT:Noether15,SP:BCGGMT14}. In each case,
users prove that their transaction is valid, and that they belong to a set of
users or hold a valid token on the ledger, without revealing which.

\paragraph{Centralisation, responsibility and trust.} In attempting to remove
trust from centralised institutions, protocols may instead transfer
computation, storage and operational responsibility to individual users. But
decentralisation alone is not a complete description of protocols' designs.

Centralisation can refer to different abilities. One is the ability to
participate in a system, which can itself mean either setting the policies that
influence how its protocols are run, or providing computation for it. Another
is the ability to see and to store the information in the system. A system can
be centralised in one of these abilities and decentralised in another.
Computation may be centralised in a party that is trusted with nothing private,
as in private outsourced computation. An entirely transparent system may be
computationally completely distributed. The parties in control of executing
verification need not be those who set the verification conditions. The
distribution of these responsibilities does not by itself determine the trust
assumptions of the system. An institution may be relied upon to provide
availability, maintain state or execute a prescribed computation without ever
having access to users' private information.

Practicality is an aim in all of our constructions. By this, we mean
minimising the interaction, coordination, communication and computational costs
required by the protocols.

Designing institutions out of systems is often not possible. Issuers,
validators and auditors exist because systems need them, and removing them from
protocols does not remove that need. Their functions are then either taken on
by users, who bear the computational costs, or moved outside the protocols,
where nothing limits what these parties learn. Privacy-focused systems are
often discussed as if the choice is between decentralised privacy and
centralised surveillance. Privacy does not automatically follow from
decentralisation, nor does it automatically disappear when corporations or
public institutions participate in a protocol. What matters is the information
made available to each participant. Powerful institutions can perform useful
protocol functions while cryptography limits both the information available to
them and the capabilities they receive. Allowing institutions to perform this
work may reduce the computation, storage, time and specialist knowledge
required of individual users. Protocol models should reflect the roles that
exist in practice.

Systems deployed today to protect user privacy already involve service
providers, issuers, auditors, validators and other accountable entities with
operational responsibilities. Privacy Pass, deployed by Cloudflare, lets users
skip repeated checks such as CAPTCHAs with tokens a server cannot link to their
issuance, and Apple's Private Relay routes traffic through two relays so that
no single one sees both a user's address and the sites they visit. Both use
institutional infrastructure while limiting the information available to the
institutions involved~\cite{PoPETS:DGSTV18,privaterelay2021}.  Modelling such
roles explicitly can enable practical and efficient systems, and increase our
understanding of the goals of private and decentralised systems.

Systems now being built have the same shape. The eIDAS regulation requires
every member state of the European Union to offer its residents a European
Digital Identity Wallet by the end of 2026~\cite{eIDAS2024}. These wallets must
support selective disclosure, unlinkability and pseudonymous authentication.
Central banks are also designing payment networks that take a very similar
shape~\cite{cbdctracker}. For the digital euro, requirements include privacy
for users, payments without a network connection, limits on holdings, and
compliance with anti-money-laundering rules through regulated
intermediaries~\cite{digitaleuro2023}.

Most of these payment networks are built on distributed ledgers. Proofs of concept
for central bank digital currencies tend to be based on distributed ledger
technology rather than conventional infrastructure~\cite{auer2020rise}, as in
the e-krona pilot~\cite{soderberg2019krona}. A ledger replicated
across several institutions stays available when any one of them fails.
Institutions then add to the ledger what a financial system needs and a cryptocurrency
lacks: registered users, endorsement by known institutions, auditing, and
limits on holdings. Offline payments are different. Two devices with no network
connection cannot consult a ledger, so the design for offline payments sits
apart from the ledger-based one. But cash works with or without a connection, so
replacing its features takes both components.

\paragraph{Financial privacy.} There are strong arguments against governments
being able to read the communications of their citizens, and companies the
messages of their customers. A private communication system can aim to ensure
that no party other than the intended recipient learns the contents of a
message.

In a financial system, the situation is different.  Constructing digital
financial systems with auditability may seem analogous to building backdoors in
messaging systems, and there is the argument that once a capability exists, it
will be used everywhere possible.

With respect to this, we raise three points. The first is that the two settings
are technically different: well-designed auditing schemes do not involve an
all-powerful entity with the ability to silently decrypt, and so the auditing
protocols in this thesis cannot be repurposed to put backdoors in encryption
schemes.  The second is that the two settings are socially different: crimes
can be organised using encrypted messaging, but what is at stake in a financial
system extends to the collapse of the global financial system. Whether that
outcome would be good or bad, it would be consequential. The third point is
that the alternative to auditable payments is not perfect privacy, but the
status quo, where banks and payment providers see all transactions in the
clear.

For both social and technical reasons, financial systems come with requirements
that private communication does not. Payment providers are required to identify
their customers and to report suspicious
transactions~\cite{fatf2012recommendations}. The systems themselves must
establish the validity of transactions, maintain a consistent shared state,
control issuance, and prevent or detect double spending, where a user spends
the same token twice~\cite{C:Chaum82,C:ChaFiaNao88}. They must remain efficient
as the number of participants grows, ideally without the computation per
participant growing linearly with the size of the system. They must also
continue to function for a user with no network
connection~\cite{aboulaiz2024offline}. We will assume throughout this thesis
that these extra parties and constraints are necessary. Their involvement makes
systems that control tokens of value more complex than private communication.

\paragraph{Registration and privacy.} Users who participate in the
financial system must be registered, but this registration must not affect the
privacy of their transactions. This can be achieved if users register once and
later prove, with each payment, that they are registered, without saying who
they are.
One way is a one-out-of-many proof, or a ring signature, over the keys of all
registered users, in which the user proves that they know the secret key for one
of the public keys in the set without revealing which. This hides the user among
all the others, but the size or cost of the proof grows with the number of
users, and the set of all keys must be published and kept up to date, which
needs infrastructure. The alternative is for a registration authority to sign
each user's key once, and for the user to prove in zero knowledge that they
hold a valid signature on their key. This is a private membership proof, and it
is what anonymous credentials already provide~\cite{EC:Chaum85,EC:CamLys01}, so
we use them for registration rather than inventing a new concept. One
construction of such a proof would use randomisable signatures that are then
proven valid in zero knowledge, such as through use of the Pointcheval-Sanders
signature scheme~\cite{RSA:PoiSan16}. This would be interactive, however, and a
proof that requires interaction with the verifier is not publicly verifiable,
and interaction can leak certain information about the users' use of the
system. Signatures on committed values avoid this: the registration authority
signs a commitment to the user's key, which enables the membership proof to be
non-interactive. Such signatures also let the authority certify a key it never
sees, and their verification is compatible with efficient zero-knowledge
proofs.

These requirements also apply to humanitarian aid. Physical cash can be lost or stolen. Many aid
recipients have no bank account, and the systems used to reach them are slow
and expensive~\cite{unicef2026aidlink}. When aid is paid through banks,
anti-money-laundering rules require the banks to check account holders against
sanctions lists, and these checks can be reviewed by financial intelligence
units and national security agencies~\cite{hayes2017migration}. Existing
digital systems for aid distribution also collect large amounts of personal
data, which can put recipients at risk~\cite{SP:WLSNT23}. Cryptocurrencies
remove the intermediaries, but move all responsibility to the users, and users
who lose their private keys lose their funds~\cite{eskandari2015bitcoin}.
UNICEF has piloted aid payments in a stablecoin that work on basic phones over
SMS, without internet access or bank accounts~\cite{unicef2026aidlink}.
Privacy-preserving aid distribution has also been built on smart cards with the
International Committee of the Red Cross~\cite{SP:WLSNT23,SP:LCSNTL25}.
Digital cash that works offline, keeps payments private and still has
accountable entities behind it could serve these recipients directly.

We take the existence of financial auditing requirements as a system constraint
and ask how narrowly the corresponding capabilities can be defined.  What
information should an auditor be able to obtain? Should access be limited to a
transaction, a user, a period of time, a single purpose, or be ongoing?

We start with the presence of these operational roles and ask how the protocols
can work with them. An institution need not be modelled in the same way for the
analysis of each property of a system. For availability and correct operation,
we assume that institutions perform their prescribed roles. For privacy, the
same institution need not be trusted to keep information available to it
confidential. It may retain and combine this information, share it with other
participants, and attempt to learn anything it can. We assess privacy against
the strongest information it can derive in this setting. Each chapter states
which participants may deviate from the protocol, and for which property.

\paragraph{The thesis.} This thesis argues that practical privacy is achieved
not by assuming away existing infrastructure, but by modelling its participants
and responsibilities accurately, and cryptographically constraining what each
participant can learn and do. In particular, an institution may perform
computation, maintain state, issue credentials, validate transactions or
provide availability without being trusted with users' private information.
Each of the protocols comes from considering the specific responsibilities of
participants in each existing system.

\paragraph{Contributions.} Each chapter of this thesis takes one role that a
financial system needs, and asks how it can be performed without learning more
than it must. The solutions are described in the introduction of the chapter
that presents them.

\Cref{chap:tsps} addresses the endorsement of transactions on a ledger. In
ledgers proposed for central bank digital currencies, several authorities each
endorse a transaction before it is recorded, while users' tokens are hidden in
commitments. The problem is how the authorities can certify a commitment
jointly, so that no single one can do so alone, without interaction slowing
down signing once a transaction arrives, and in a form that users can prove
statements about in zero knowledge.

\Cref{chap:agg-creds} addresses the certification of users' attributes. In
practice, different institutions each certify a few attributes and do not
coordinate with one another. Aggregating their credentials is motivated by
efficiency: presenting and verifying them together is more efficient than
presenting each credential alone. The problem is how a user can present
attributes from independent issuers together, without any issuer learning the attributes
of others, and with credentials that cannot be pooled between users.

\Cref{chap:txid} addresses auditing. Regulators need to investigate
transactions, but an auditor with a general ability to trace users learns the
transactions of every user. The problem is how to limit an auditor to one user and
one period of time, only after the user has granted permission, without forcing
users to create their transactions one after another.

\Cref{chap:offline-payments} addresses payments without a network connection.
A payee who is offline cannot check whether a token has already been spent. The
problem is how to prevent double spending, and to identify the double spender
when prevention fails, while the payments of honest users remain private and
the computation required stays within what a secure element can do.

\paragraph{Layout.} The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
Preliminary information is found in \Cref{chap:preliminaries}. \Cref{chap:tsps}
presents our threshold structure-preserving signature schemes.
\Cref{chap:agg-creds} presents the aggregate anonymous credential scheme,
\Cref{chap:txid} presents the transaction tracing protocol, and
\Cref{chap:offline-payments} presents the offline payment protocol.  The
chapters start by considering certification, then move to explore transaction
auditing, and then finally we present a system that integrates the previously
explored concepts and adds the ability to make offline payments. Each of
\Crefrange{chap:tsps}{chap:offline-payments} follows the same structure after
its introduction: definitions, construction, instantiation, security,
evaluation, and a summary.  \Cref{chap:conclusion} summarises the contributions
and discusses open questions.
\Cref{chap:agg-creds} uses the $\AGHO$ SPS scheme from \Cref{chap:tsps}, and
\Cref{chap:txid,chap:offline-payments} share the registration authority and
BBS+ credentials. The threshold techniques of \Cref{chap:tsps} could be used to
split the authorities in the later chapters between several institutions.
