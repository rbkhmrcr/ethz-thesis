\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

The desire to conceal written information has been considered for at least the
last four thousand years, from the obfuscated hieroglyphs found in the tomb of
Khnumhotep II~\cite{Kahn96}. More recently, encryption schemes have let people communicate by
exchanging messages that reveal nothing about their content to eavesdroppers. Parties with a shared key can send each other
confidential messages, regardless of the content of those messages, and
independently of the power of, language spoken by, or any other attributes of
the communicating parties. Modern cryptography has enabled encrypted
communication that even truly powerful entities cannot read.

The communicating parties are not assumed to be as powerful as the
entities trying to break the system -- encryption demands relatively little
computational power of the users while keeping the adversary's computational
task infeasible. This asymmetry of computational power between users
and attacking entities is well noted, and is the foundation of modern
cryptography~\cite{DifHel76}.

However, protecting the content of a message says nothing about who is
exchanging it, when, or how often. Once the contents of a communication are protected, a resourceful
adversary may instead try to learn the identities of the communicating parties,
their relationships, or patterns in their behaviour. Protecting each of these
brings the next piece of observable information into focus.

Cryptography today covers much more than private communication. Over the last
fifty years, privacy-enhancing technologies have been developed
to protect each new piece of information identified as valuable~\cite{Chaum81,USENIX:DinMatSyv04}. As the adoption
of digital systems has grown, even of those with privacy protections, so has the
number of entities capable of observing some part of a system. Service
providers, issuers, validators, network operators and public authorities may all
learn sensitive information while performing otherwise legitimate functions.
This raises the question of how these participants should be incorporated into
the system model, and what information they should be permitted to learn.

These entities are often modelled as adversaries.
Protocol designers have understandably been active in designing systems that
take power away from centralised entities. Anything that such an
entity learns is considered a step away from the ideal world.

\paragraph{Centralisation, responsibility and trust.} In attempting to remove
trust from centralised institutions, protocols may instead transfer computation,
storage and operational responsibility to individual users. But centralisation
is not a single property, and decentralisation alone is not a complete
description of protocols' designs.

Centralisation can refer to different abilities. One is the ability to
participate in a system, which can itself mean either setting the policies that
influence how its protocols are run, or providing computation for it. Another is
the ability to see and to store the information in the system. A system can be
centralised in one of these abilities and decentralised in another. Computation
may be centralised in a party that is trusted with nothing private, as in
private outsourced computation. An entirely transparent system may be
computationally completely distributed. The parties in control of executing
verification need not be those who set the verification conditions. The
distribution of these responsibilities does not by itself determine the trust
assumptions of the system. An institution may be relied upon to provide
availability, maintain state or execute a prescribed computation without ever
having access to users' private information. Anonymous credentials and
multiparty computation are two examples.

In anonymous credential schemes, issuers issue credentials to users, attesting
that users hold certain attribute values. Users present their credentials,
proving they hold given attributes without revealing who they are or anything
about their undisclosed attribute values~\cite{EC:Chaum85,EC:CamLys01}. Early anonymous credential schemes
assumed a single issuer certifying all attributes, creating one powerful entity.
Later schemes decentralised issuance, but the issuers as a whole remain
responsible for all attributes, so that all issuers can now learn all users'
attribute values~\cite{NDSS:SABMD19}. The power to certify and the knowledge that comes with that
have both been shared across issuers, which leads to a worse privacy outcome for
users.

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) allows a function to be computed by multiple mutually
distrusting parties, each with a private input. The function is computed on all
inputs while revealing nothing but the output~\cite{FOCS:Yao86,STOC:GolMicWig87}. This seems to be an instance
where decentralisation of the protocol leads to a straightforward privacy gain
for users. It can be applied to anonymous credential issuance to sidestep the
issue stated above. The new problem it introduces, however, is in practicality.
Multiparty computation generally involves interaction from all participants,
which becomes infeasible when, for example, a driving licence, a birth
certificate and a passport are each issued by countries with no diplomatic
relations with one another. These issuers have no incentive to participate in an
expensive protocol to allow individuals to privately prove statements about
themselves. And so practicality is also an aim in all of our constructions. By
this, we mean minimising the interaction, coordination, communication and
computational costs required by the protocols.

Designing institutions out of systems is often not possible. Issuers,
validators and auditors exist because systems need them, and removing them from
protocols does not remove that need. Their functions are then either taken on by
users, who bear the computational costs, or moved outside the protocols, where
nothing limits what these parties learn. Privacy-focused systems are often
discussed as if the choice is between decentralised privacy and centralised
surveillance. Privacy does not automatically
follow from decentralisation, nor does it automatically disappear when
corporations or public institutions participate in a protocol. What matters is
the information made available to each participant. Powerful institutions can
perform useful protocol functions while cryptography limits both the information
available to them and the capabilities they receive. Allowing institutions to
perform this work may reduce the computation, storage, time and specialist
knowledge required of individual users. Protocol models should reflect the roles
that exist in practice.

Systems deployed today to protect user privacy already involve service
providers, issuers, auditors, validators and other accountable entities with
operational responsibilities. Privacy Pass, deployed by Cloudflare, lets users skip repeated checks such as
CAPTCHAs with tokens a server cannot link to their issuance, and Apple's Private
Relay routes traffic through two relays so that no single one sees both a user's
address and the sites they visit. Both use institutional infrastructure while
limiting the information available to the institutions involved~\cite{PoPETS:DGSTV18,privaterelay2021}.
Modelling such roles explicitly can enable practical and efficient systems, and
increase our understanding of the goals of private and decentralised systems.

Systems now being built have the same shape. Every member state of the European
Union must offer its residents a European Digital Identity Wallet by the end of
2026~\cite{eIDAS2024}. These wallets must support selective disclosure,
unlinkability and pseudonymous authentication. Central banks are also designing
payment networks that take a very similar shape~\cite{cbdctracker}. For the
digital euro, requirements include privacy for users, payments without a network
connection, limits on holdings, and compliance with anti-money-laundering rules
through regulated intermediaries~\cite{digitaleuro2023}.

\paragraph{Financial privacy.} There are extremely strong arguments against
governments being able to read the communications of their citizens, and
companies the messages of their customers. A private communication system can
aim to ensure that no party other than the intended recipient learns the
contents of a message.

In a financial system, the situation is different.
Constructing digital financial systems with auditability may seem analogous to
building backdoors in messaging systems, and there is the argument that once a
capability exists, it will be used everywhere possible.

With respect to this, we raise three points. The first is that the two settings
are technically different: well-designed auditing schemes do not involve an
all-powerful entity with the ability to silently decrypt, and so the auditing
protocols in this thesis cannot be repurposed to put backdoors in encryption
schemes.
The second is that the two settings are socially different: crimes can be
organised using encrypted messaging, but what is at stake in a financial system
extends to the collapse of the global financial system. Whether that outcome
would be good or bad, it would be consequential. The third point is that the alternative to auditable payments is
not perfect privacy, but the status quo, where banks and payment providers see
all transactions in the clear.

For both social and technical reasons, financial systems come with requirements
that private communication does not. Payment providers are required to identify
their customers and to report suspicious
transactions~\cite{fatf2012recommendations}. The systems themselves must establish the validity of
transactions, maintain a consistent shared state, control issuance, and prevent
or detect double spending, where a user spends the same token
twice~\cite{C:Chaum82,C:ChaFiaNao88}. They must remain
efficient as the number of participants grows, ideally without cost per
participant growing linearly with the size of the system. They must also
continue to function for a user with no network
connection~\cite{aboulaiz2024offline}. We will assume throughout this thesis
that these extra parties and constraints are necessary. Their involvement makes
systems that control tokens of value more complex than private communication.

We take the existence of financial auditing requirements as a system
constraint and asks how narrowly the corresponding capabilities can be defined.
What information should an auditor be able to obtain? Should access be limited
to a transaction, a user, a period of time, a single purpose, or be ongoing?

We start with the presence of these operational roles and ask how the protocols
can work with them. An institution need not be modelled in the same way for the
analysis of each property of a system. For availability and correct operation,
we assume that institutions perform their prescribed roles. For privacy, the
same institution need not be trusted to keep information available to it
confidential. It may retain and combine this information, share it with other
participants, and attempt to learn anything it can. We assess privacy against
the strongest information it can derive in this setting. Each chapter states which participants may deviate from the
protocol, and for which property.

\paragraph{The thesis.} This thesis argues that practical privacy is achieved
not by assuming away existing infrastructure, but by modelling its participants
and responsibilities accurately, and cryptographically constraining what each
participant can learn and do. In particular, an institution may perform
computation, maintain state, issue credentials, validate transactions or provide
availability without being trusted with users' private information. Each of the
protocols comes from considering the specific responsibilities of participants
in each existing system.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We first consider threshold certification, by which we
mean the threshold signing of commitments. We achieve this by constructing a framework for creating
threshold structure-preserving signature schemes. Structure-preserving signature
schemes allow signers to sign commitments directly, and threshold versions allow
us to distribute the signing responsibilities across several parties. We give a
generic construction which splits signing into offline and online phases, in
order to remove communication requirements between signers entirely in the
online phase. We instantiate the construction with two signature schemes, and
implement and evaluate both instantiations with two MPC protocols.

We then consider aggregate anonymous credentials. Existing credential models do
not always reflect the way that authority is divided in practice. Users may
receive attributes from several independent issuers and later need to
demonstrate statements about subsets of them. We focus on decentralisation of
issuance. We construct an efficient multi-issuer aggregate anonymous credential
scheme in which independently issued credentials can be combined and presented
through a single proof. Anonymous credential schemes already implicitly include
a registration authority. We use this existing role to bind credentials issued
to the same user, preventing credentials belonging to different users from being
aggregated without requiring expensive proofs during presentation.

We next consider accountability in transaction systems that protect user
privacy. Financial systems may require both the ability to investigate a
particular transaction and the ability to identify the transactions associated
with a particular user. These capabilities are related but distinct, and
granting one need not grant the other. We take the auditor's existence as given
and ask whether its capability can be scoped like any other permission, to one
user and one period, and only after an explicit grant. We
develop a transaction identification mechanism that supports concurrent
transaction creation without requiring the auditor to maintain persistent
per-user state. Auditing is restricted to a specified epoch, a fixed period of time, limiting both the
scope and duration of the information disclosed to an auditor.

Finally, we study privacy in offline payments. Offline operation improves
availability and accessibility, but changes how double spending must be handled.
We develop an offline payment scheme with two defences against double spending.
The first is prevention: we divide computation between a mobile device and a
secure element, a tamper-resistant chip such as those in bank cards, and the secure element does not allow the same value to be spent
twice. The second is detection: if a secure element is broken and double
spending occurs, information identifying the double spender can be recovered.
Payments made by honest users remain unlinkable.
